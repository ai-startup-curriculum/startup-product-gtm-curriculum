# Exercise 04 — MEDDIC / MEDDPICC Qualification Drill

**Estimated time:** 3 hours
**Chapter link:** [`04-enterprise-meddic-meddpicc-motion.md`](../04-enterprise-meddic-meddpicc-motion.md)
**Prerequisite:** Chapter 4 read end-to-end; access to at least one real enterprise-shaped opportunity (open or closed, won or lost) — or a plausible reconstructed case from your discovery corpus (mod-101) or from a public case study of a comparable deal. If you have never worked an enterprise deal, use a hypothetical based on your ICP + ACV scaled up (e.g., "if we sold to a Fortune 500 subsidiary at $400K ACV, what would the MEDDPICC coverage look like?"). Label the source of the deal explicitly.

## Problem statement

Chapter 4 named MEDDIC / MEDDPICC as the operating qualification framework for enterprise SaaS sales, drew on John McMahon's *The Qualified Sales Leader* and Andy Whyte's *MEDDICC: The Ultimate Guide*, and named the failure mode: founders port the mid-market SPICED motion to enterprise deals without upgrading it — no Metrics document, no Economic-buyer meeting, no Decision-process mapping, no Paper-process forecast, no Competition mapping — and enterprise deals close-lose at the last minute on "our CFO said no" or slip a quarter every quarter for a year.

This exercise trains you to **build the MEDDPICC coverage on a specific enterprise deal, letter by letter, honestly graded, with the artifacts that back each letter and the gaps that force follow-up work**. The deliverable is not a passing grade on all eight letters; it is an *honest* diagnostic that names which letters are green with evidence, which are yellow with visible gaps, and which are red — and the specific next-action work to close the reds and yellows.

The failure mode this exercise exists to catch: **the sales team marks every MEDDPICC letter as "green" because the panel exists in the CRM and marking-it-green feels like progress — producing a decorative panel that has zero diagnostic value and forecasts a deal at 90% probability three days before it close-losts on a letter that was actually red**.

## Requirements

Deliver a folder `exercise-04/` with three files:

- `part-a-deal-scoping.md` — the deal shape, the stakeholder map, the source of the case.
- `part-b-meddpicc-coverage.md` — letter-by-letter coverage with evidence, gaps, and grade.
- `part-c-champion-brief-and-action-plan.md` — the champion-enablement one-pager and the next-action plan to close remaining reds/yellows.

### Part A — Deal scoping and stakeholder map (45 min)

Deliver `part-a-deal-scoping.md`.

**A1 — Deal shape (10 min).** Name the deal in the four Chapter 4 enterprise criteria:

- **ACV** — $ per year, and confirm it crosses the $100K threshold that puts it in enterprise territory. If < $100K, note it and clarify whether you're running MEDDPICC as an over-invested exercise or MEDDIC-lite on a mid-market-plus deal.
- **Cycle length** — expected total time from first meeting to signed contract. Enterprise default is 6-18 months; note where in the band the deal falls.
- **Buying committee size** — expected number of stakeholders across expected roles.
- **Formal process** — is there an RFP, security questionnaire, redlined MSA, procurement engagement expected?

**A2 — Deal source and provenance (5 min).** Label the source of the deal:

- **Real open deal** — currently in your pipeline. Preferred; the exercise produces immediately-usable output.
- **Real closed deal (won or lost)** — historical retro. Useful for pattern-recognition; note whether the retro is honest about what actually happened.
- **Reconstructed from discovery corpus (mod-101)** — a plausible enterprise deal built from your discovery interviews.
- **Public case study** — from a comparable vendor's blog / conference talk / SaaStr session. Cite the source URL.
- **Hypothetical scale-up** — you have never worked an enterprise deal; you are running MEDDPICC as a thought experiment on what a scaled-up version of your ICP would look like.

**A3 — Stakeholder map (30 min).** Enumerate the buying committee stakeholders. For each stakeholder, list:

- **Name** (if known, or role + hiring context if hypothetical).
- **Role** — Economic Buyer / Champion / Technical Evaluator / User / Procurement / Legal / Security / Finance / Executive Sponsor / Blocker (per Chapter 4's taxonomy).
- **Known KPIs / motivations** — what does this stakeholder care about; what does she measure her own success on; what would win her support; what would lose it.
- **Contact status** — met (yes/no); met by whom on the vendor side; last touch date.
- **Blocker risk** — the specific reason this stakeholder might block the deal (competing vendor allegiance; preferred incumbent; security concerns; budget priorities).

At minimum, the map covers the Economic Buyer, the Champion, and the Technical Evaluator; complete enterprise deals typically have 5-15 stakeholders. Include your best hypothesis on the Blocker even if not yet identified — Chapter 4's fifth-role hidden-veto pattern.

### Part B — MEDDPICC coverage, letter by letter (90 min)

Deliver `part-b-meddpicc-coverage.md`. For each of the eight letters, write a coverage entry:

```
## {Letter} — {full name}

**Grade**: Green / Yellow / Red

**Evidence**:
- {specific artifact, meeting, document, or statement that supports the grade — with date and source}
- {second piece of evidence}
- ...

**Gap**:
- {what is missing to move to a higher grade}

**Next action**:
- {specific work item — meeting to book, document to write, stakeholder to engage — with owner and target date}
```

Cover all eight letters:

**B1 — M — Metrics.** The specific quantitative outcomes the buyer expects. Not "improve productivity" but "reduce X by Y%, saving $Z/year." The Metrics document is the ROI story the champion uses internally.

**B2 — E — Economic Buyer.** The person with veto authority. Identified by name; ideally met at least once. Understood in terms of her KPIs and decision criteria (which usually differ from the champion's).

**B3 — D — Decision Criteria.** The stated criteria the buyer will use to compare vendors — technical, security, commercial, qualitative. Explicit list; each item either met, re-negotiated, or acknowledged as a fit gap.

**B4 — D — Decision Process.** The sequence of steps from "we're interested" to "signed contract." Named stages, named owners, approximate durations.

**B5 — I — Identify Pain.** Multi-stakeholder pain characterisation — champion's pain (career-adjacent), user's pain (day-to-day), Economic Buyer's pain (KPI/P&L), security's pain (compliance risk). Each pain maps to a component of the Metrics story.

**B6 — C — Champion.** Identified by name; explicitly trained on how to sell the deal internally; given the materials she needs (ROI doc, case studies, internal talking points, objection-handling one-pager). Trained-champion signal is the single strongest predictor of enterprise close.

**B7 — P — Paper Process.** The contract/redline/procurement/legal process — MSA template selection, SOW drafting, redline cycles, procurement approval, e-signature routing, countersign. Enterprise legal typically takes 6-16 weeks; deals under-managed on Paper Process slip a quarter each time an unexpected redline surfaces.

**B8 — C — Competition.** Incumbent solution + alternative vendors under evaluation + the "do nothing" option. Characterised specifically — not "we have competition" but "vendor X is incumbent, mid-renewal, champion prefers us on Y, security prefers them on Z, CFO anchored to their price of $W."

**Grading discipline.** Chapter 5's rule for the MEDDPICC panel:

- **Green** — evidence exists on the opportunity and can be cited to a specific document, meeting, or artifact.
- **Yellow** — partial coverage; evidence exists but has a specific gap (Champion identified but not yet trained; Metrics sketched but not sized in $).
- **Red** — no coverage; the letter has not been engaged.

At the end of B8, produce a summary line:

```
Coverage summary: M {G/Y/R} | E {G/Y/R} | D {G/Y/R} | D {G/Y/R} | I {G/Y/R} | C {G/Y/R} | P {G/Y/R} | C {G/Y/R}
Stage this deal is in per Chapter 5: {Discovery / Evaluation / Proposal / Negotiation / Close}
Stage-exit criteria satisfied per Chapter 5? {yes/no + which letters block advancement}
```

### Part C — Champion brief and action plan (45 min)

Deliver `part-c-champion-brief-and-action-plan.md`.

**C1 — Champion enablement one-pager (25 min).** Chapter 4 names the champion as a *trained asset* — she needs the ROI document, the case studies, the internal talking points, and the objection-handling one-pager to sell the deal to the Economic Buyer, Procurement, Security, and Finance without you in the room.

Author a one-pager the champion could hand-forward to any stakeholder. Structure:

```
# Loomly for {Buyer Company} — {Date}

## The problem
- {2-3 sentences describing the pain in the buyer's own language}
- {Specific KPI or workflow this pain affects}

## The proposed solution
- {2-3 sentences on how Loomly addresses the pain}
- {The specific outcome — measurable — the buyer can expect}

## ROI summary
- {The Metrics story — quantified in $ or hours or %}
- {The confidence band around the metric — "based on our POC data" or "based on customer X's public results"}

## How it fits with existing tools
- {Integration story — what stays, what goes, what changes}
- {Migration cost estimate}

## Commercial terms
- {Anchor pricing — ACV, contract length, key commercial terms}
- {Assumptions being made}

## Anticipated objections and responses
- {Objection 1 — likely from CFO / Security / Procurement / Legal}
    - Response: {2-3 sentences}
- {Objection 2 — likely from Technical Evaluator or User}
    - Response: {2-3 sentences}
- {Objection 3 — competitor allegiance or incumbent preference}
    - Response: {2-3 sentences}

## Next step
- {What the champion asks the Economic Buyer to do — a specific meeting, a decision, an approval}

## Reference points
- {Case study of comparable buyer, if available}
- {Named customer references willing to take a call}
```

The champion should be able to send this document to her CTO or CFO without editing. If it reads like a vendor pitch deck, it will not survive internal forwarding.

**C2 — Next-action plan to close reds and yellows (20 min).** For each red or yellow letter from Part B, name:

- **Action** — the specific work item.
- **Owner** — AE / SE / Founder / Champion (with explicit ask if the owner is the Champion).
- **Target date** — a specific date, not "soon."
- **Success criterion** — how you'll know the action moved the grade.

Prioritise. Chapter 4 names Champion and Economic Buyer as the highest-leverage letters — a Yellow Champion or a Yellow Economic Buyer usually blocks stage-advancement per Chapter 5. Sequence the actions so the load-bearing letters get worked first.

## Starter guidance

- **Grade honestly.** Chapter 5's *MEDDPICC-panel-as-decoration* trap is exactly the failure this exercise trains against. If the Champion has been identified but not yet met, that's Yellow, not Green. If the Metrics number is a slide-deck talking point without a $ number sized to the buyer's actual environment, that's Yellow. Green requires *evidence*, not intent.
- **Every "green" needs an artifact.** For each Green grade, cite a specific document, meeting, or written communication. A Green without an artifact is a Yellow the AE is over-selling.
- **The Champion is a role, not a job title.** A Champion is (a) genuinely wants the outcome, (b) has credibility with the Economic Buyer, and (c) is willing to spend political capital. A Director who thinks the product is "interesting" but won't advocate internally is not a Champion; she is an interested contact. Downgrade honestly.
- **Economic Buyer requires *direct engagement*.** "The Champion says the CFO is aligned" is not Economic Buyer coverage — it is Champion coverage of what the Champion believes about the CFO. Green Economic Buyer requires at least one direct meeting or direct written communication with the actual Economic Buyer.
- **Metrics without a $ number is decorative.** "Improve productivity" is a Yellow at best. "Reduce mean-time-to-resolve by 40% saving 2,000 engineer-hours/year at $200/hour = $400K/year" is a Green Metrics. The finance team needs a number they can put in a spreadsheet; the champion needs a number she can quote to her CFO.
- **Decision Process mapping is what prevents quarterly slippage.** Deals slip because someone forgot to ask "and then what?" at each step. Author the Decision Process as a specific sequence: technical eval → POC → business case → procurement → legal → executive sign-off → countersign, with an estimated duration per step. Update as new steps are surfaced.
- **Paper Process is often the longest stage.** 6-16 weeks of enterprise legal is standard; complex deals stretch to 12-24 weeks. Under-forecasted paper process is the number-one reason enterprise deals miss the forecasted close date. Grade P honestly and forecast the close date against the *paper process* estimate, not the champion's "we want to be done by end of quarter."
- **Competition includes "do nothing."** The most common enterprise loss is not to a competitor — it is to no decision. Chapter 4 explicitly names "do nothing" as a Competition option. Include it in your mapping if applicable.
- **The champion one-pager is what the deal actually turns on.** In the moments you are not in the room, the champion is selling with what you gave her. A great one-pager makes her sound credible; a bad one-pager makes her sound like she is quoting a vendor. Draft C1 with the specific champion's voice and internal-political-environment in mind.
- **Sequencing matters in the action plan.** Chapter 5 names Economic Buyer and Champion as the letters that block stage-advancement most often. If those two letters are Yellow, prioritise moving them to Green *before* investing time in Yellow Decision Criteria or Yellow Paper Process — those latter letters are downstream and will not advance if the leading letters are not green.
- **If the deal is hypothetical, still be specific.** A hypothetical Fortune 500 subsidiary at $400K ACV with a hypothetical CTO Economic Buyer still requires plausible KPIs, plausible pain, plausible decision criteria. "Hypothetical" is not permission to hand-wave; it is permission to name assumptions.

## Acceptance criteria

Your submission is complete when:

- [ ] Part A names the deal shape (ACV, cycle length, buying committee size, formal process expected) and confirms enterprise-tier fit.
- [ ] Part A labels the deal source explicitly (real open / real closed / reconstructed / public case study / hypothetical) with citation if applicable.
- [ ] Part A enumerates the stakeholder map with role (per Chapter 4 taxonomy), known KPIs / motivations, contact status, and blocker risk. At minimum: Economic Buyer, Champion, Technical Evaluator, plus at least one Blocker hypothesis.
- [ ] Part B provides a letter-by-letter coverage entry for all 8 MEDDPICC letters, each with grade (G/Y/R), evidence, gap, and next action.
- [ ] Part B applies grading discipline honestly — Green requires cited artifacts; Yellow names the specific gap; Red is used where coverage does not exist.
- [ ] Part B ends with a coverage-summary line, the current Chapter 5 stage the deal is in, and whether the stage-exit criteria for the next stage are met (naming which letters block advancement if not).
- [ ] Part C's champion one-pager is authored in the given structure and readable to a non-technical Economic Buyer without vendor-side context.
- [ ] Part C's next-action plan names actions with owner, target date, and success criterion for each red or yellow letter, sequenced to prioritise Champion and Economic Buyer letters.
- [ ] The champion one-pager includes at least 3 anticipated objections with responses (CFO / Security / Procurement / Legal / Technical Evaluator / User / competitor allegiance).
- [ ] Any hypothetical deal element (invented stakeholder KPIs, invented pain sizings, invented pricing terms) is labelled as such rather than reported as fact.
- [ ] Any factual claim (a benchmark rate, an industry standard, a competitor product feature) that is not from Chapter 4's cited sources is flagged `<!-- needs-research: ... -->` rather than invented.

## Common ways this exercise goes wrong

- **All-Green trap.** Every letter is graded Green because the AE feels good about the deal. Panel is decorative; no diagnostic value. Fix: grade against the evidence rule — Green requires a citable artifact; if you can't cite, downgrade.
- **Champion-inflation trap.** An interested Director is labelled Champion. She has never advocated internally; she has never taken the ROI doc to her CFO. The deal is Champion-Green but functionally Champion-Yellow at best. Fix: three-part Champion test — wants the outcome, has credibility with EB, willing to spend political capital. Failing any one = Yellow at best.
- **Economic-Buyer-by-proxy trap.** EB is graded Green because the Champion assures you the CFO is aligned. You have never met the CFO. Deal close-losts at the last stage when the CFO turns out to have different priorities. Fix: EB Green requires direct engagement; the Champion's belief about the EB is not EB coverage.
- **Metrics-as-adjectives trap.** M grade is Green with "significant time savings" or "meaningful productivity improvement." No $ number, no confidence band, no source. Fix: M Green requires a quantified ROI with a source (POC data, comparable customer data, buyer's own KPI targets).
- **Decision-Process-as-hope trap.** DP is Green with "the champion says end-of-quarter." No named steps, no owners, no durations. Fix: DP Green requires a named sequence with duration estimates; "hoping to close by X" is not a Decision Process.
- **Paper-Process-blindness trap.** P is graded Green because "we sent the MSA." No knowledge of the buyer's legal-review-cycle-time, no procurement engagement, no e-signature routing plan. Fix: P Green requires visibility into the buyer's specific paper process, not just "we sent our template."
- **Competition-as-generic trap.** C is graded Green with "we have some competitors." No named competitor, no positioning, no incumbent-vs-us on specific criteria. Fix: C Green requires named competitors with specific positioning per criterion; unmapped competition = Yellow at best.
- **Blocker-missing trap.** Stakeholder map covers named EB, Champion, TE — but no Blocker hypothesis. The Blocker is the fifth-role hidden veto (Chapter 4 + mod-105 Chapter 3). Fix: even without a named Blocker, include a hypothesis in Part A ("likely blocker: security lead with allegiance to incumbent vendor; not yet identified by name").
- **Champion-one-pager-reads-as-vendor-pitch trap.** C1 uses vendor buzzwords, features-over-outcomes, and marketing tone. Champion cannot forward without editing. Fix: write in the buyer's language; use "we" as if the champion is the author; include the objections a real internal reader would raise.
- **Action-plan-without-owners trap.** C2 lists "close Economic Buyer gap" without saying who does what by when. No accountability; nothing happens. Fix: every action has owner and date; every action has a success criterion.
- **Sequencing-inverted trap.** C2 prioritises easy actions (update the Metrics doc) over hard load-bearing actions (get the EB meeting). Load-bearing letters stay Yellow. Fix: sequence by leverage; Champion and EB are the letters that block stage-advancement most often per Chapter 5.
- **Hypothetical-as-hand-wave trap.** Part A labels the deal hypothetical and then uses hypothesis as permission to skip specifics. Names are omitted, KPIs are hand-waved, pain is generic. Fix: hypothetical requires plausible specifics with assumptions labelled; hypothesis is not permission to be vague.
- **Grade-in-isolation trap.** Coverage is graded letter-by-letter with no cross-letter reconciliation. Metrics is Green but Metrics has no source in Identify Pain (the ROI number was invented, not derived from a stakeholder's pain). Fix: cross-check — Metrics traces to Identify Pain; Champion enables Metrics; Economic Buyer approves the Metrics number; Decision Criteria includes the Metrics as a requirement.
- **No-Chapter-5-tie-in trap.** Part B coverage exists but is not linked to Chapter 5's stage model. AE can't answer "which stage should this deal be in?" or "what letters block advancement?" Fix: end Part B with the stage-summary line naming current stage and blocking letters.
