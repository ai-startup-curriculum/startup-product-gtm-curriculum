# The Three Positioning Failure Modes

## Motivation

Chapter 2 gave you the five-component frame; this chapter gives you the diagnostic that tells you when the frame has been applied incorrectly. Dunford names three failure modes explicitly in *Obviously Awesome*, each of which produces a distinct, visible symptom on a live product page or in a sales-team win-loss analysis. The failure modes are diagnosable *from the outside* — you do not need internal access; a product page, a pitch deck, and a few sales calls are enough to identify which mode a company is in.

The reason to name the modes is the same reason mod-102 named the four PMF failure modes: **the mode determines the remedy.** Obvious-but-underwhelming is fixed by re-selecting the alternatives (Chapter 2 Component 1); "we're better" is fixed by tracing value from attributes (Component 3); category confusion is fixed by re-selecting the market category (Component 5). A team that cannot name which mode they are in ends up rewriting the tagline for the third time and moving nothing.

This chapter names the modes, gives the diagnostic, and shows how each mode reads on a real product page.

## Core concepts

### The three modes at a glance

| Mode | The claim | What it looks like on the page | The fix component |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Obvious-but-underwhelming** | Positioned against the wrong alternatives; the differentiation is real but only matters against alternatives no one is comparing you to. | Hero copy reads as "we're a slightly different X" against an incumbent no one is buying anyway; buyers get to the page, understand what you are, and leave because they're not shopping in that comparison set. | Component 1 (alternatives) |
| **2. "We're better"** | Feature parity language without traceable value; positioned as an incrementally better version of the incumbent. | Hero copy is a comparative superlative ("faster / cheaper / better / easier") without naming the underlying attribute that produces the "faster." Product page is a checklist of features that duplicates the incumbent's checklist. | Component 3 (value derived from attributes) |
| **3. Category confusion** | Buyer cannot tell which mental shelf you belong on; the category label is invented, vague, or contradicts the actual buyer. | Hero copy uses a category label that produces no search intent ("productivity fabric," "AI-native workspace," "workflow platform"). Sales cycle stalls because prospects can't route the deal to the right internal buyer. | Component 5 (market category) |

The three modes are not mutually exclusive — a single product page can exhibit two or all three simultaneously, and often does. The diagnostic below teaches you to name each mode separately even when they stack.

### Mode 1 — obvious-but-underwhelming

**Dunford's framing:** *the positioning is obvious — the buyer immediately understands what the product is — but the differentiation is underwhelming because the product is compared against alternatives that don't matter to the buyer.* ([April Dunford — *Obviously Awesome*, Chapter 3](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome).)

**The mechanism:** the founder built the positioning against the direct in-category competitors. The comparison set on the page is "Salesforce vs. us; HubSpot vs. us; Zoho vs. us." Every attribute is named as "we're the one that also does X but with less setup." The buyer arrives on the page. The buyer is not currently on Salesforce — they are on a spreadsheet. The Salesforce comparison is irrelevant to them. The differentiation is real but pointed at the wrong reference point, so it fails to land.

**Diagnostic signals:**
- The product page compares itself to two or three direct competitors, all major incumbents in the category.
- The hero copy uses formulations like "the modern alternative to X" or "everything Y does, but simpler."
- Sales-call transcripts reveal that most prospects, when asked "what were you using before?", name a spreadsheet, a homegrown tool, or "nothing" — but the pitch deck spends slides comparing to Salesforce.
- The team's win-loss analysis shows most losses are to "no decision" (the status quo), not to the named competitors.
- Free-form conversations with prospects surface the phrase "yeah I get it, we're just not really in that market."

**Why the mode is dangerous:** it feels *correct*. The founder has done positioning work, has named alternatives, has articulated differentiators. The page is coherent. The problem is invisible because it is a problem of *reference point* — you cannot fix it by rewriting words on the current positioning; you have to change what the words are pointing at.

**Fix:** run Chapter 2 Component 1 correctly. Interview five to ten actual buyers with the *"what would you do without our product?"* question. The words they say are the alternatives set. Re-author the entire positioning against the true alternatives. Hero copy, attribute list, and comparison table on the page all rewrite accordingly.

### Mode 2 — "we're better"

**Dunford's framing:** *the product is positioned as an incrementally better version of the incumbent — same feature list, same category, just "better."* Without a traceable value proposition that identifies which unique attribute produces the "better," the claim collapses into noise. ([April Dunford — *Obviously Awesome*, Chapter 3](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome); reprised in [April Dunford — "The three styles of positioning" (blog)](https://www.aprildunford.com/blog).)

**The mechanism:** the product-page hero is a comparative superlative ("faster," "cheaper," "simpler," "easier," "the modern X"). The feature list is a parity checklist against the incumbent — everything they have, we have too. There is no *unique attribute* named on the page, and consequently no *specific value* that traces to one. The buyer reads the page as "this is a me-too version of Salesforce, minus Salesforce's brand recognition, minus Salesforce's ecosystem, minus Salesforce's security certifications. Why would I take that risk?"

**Diagnostic signals:**
- The hero copy has a comparative-adjective structure ("faster / cheaper / better / simpler") without naming *what* structural property makes it so.
- The product page has a feature-comparison table with the incumbent, and the table is a sea of green checkmarks on your side and a mix on theirs — every feature they have, you also have. This is *parity*, not differentiation.
- The word "just" appears in the hero: "the same X, just [adjective]-er."
- Value bullets on the product page are claims ("save 40% on X"; "10× faster deployments") without traceable attribute causation. The number is asserted but not derived.
- Sales calls where the prospect says: "okay, so what is the *actual* difference?" This is the sales-team-facing signal.

**Why the mode is dangerous:** it is the default output of a marketing team who was handed "differentiate us from the incumbent" without a positioning workshop. Every comparative adjective is easier to write than the underlying attribute → value chain, so this mode is *cheaper to produce* than the correct positioning. The team ships it and confuses "we have marketing copy" with "we have positioning."

**Fix:** run Chapter 2 Components 2 and 3. For every "we're X-er" claim, write down the specific *unique attribute* that produces the "X-er" and the *value chain* from attribute to buyer consequence. If the chain cannot be written, the claim is not defensible and must be replaced. The rewritten hero names the *cause*, not the *effect*.

### Mode 3 — category confusion

**Dunford's framing:** *the buyer cannot tell what category the product belongs to, so they cannot decide whether they need it, who inside their organisation should look at it, or which incumbents to compare it to.* The buyer gets to the page, cannot file the product on any existing mental shelf, and leaves. ([April Dunford — *Obviously Awesome*, Chapter 4](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome).)

**The mechanism:** the founder either invented a category label ("developer productivity fabric," "AI-native workspace," "revenue operations platform"), used a label so generic it names nothing ("collaboration platform," "workflow engine"), or used a label from an adjacent-but-wrong category ("we're a project management tool" — when the actual use case is CRM). The buyer's mental shelf-lookup fails: no shelf has this label; the label matches five different shelves; or the label matches the wrong shelf. The buyer has no way to compare the product to anything, no way to price it, and no way to route the decision inside their organisation.

**Diagnostic signals:**
- The hero copy uses a category label that is either (a) unique to the company (nobody else uses this label), (b) generic to the point of meaninglessness ("platform," "solution," "operating system for X"), or (c) mismatched (the label routes to one buyer, the actual use case routes to another).
- The company appears in no analyst report or industry publication under any consistent category name.
- Sales cycles are unusually long because the prospect internally cannot decide who owns the buying decision — the deal bounces between the VP Eng, the VP Sales, and the CIO, none of whom see it as clearly theirs.
- Google search traffic to the site is either zero (the invented category has no search volume) or comes from queries so broad the visitors are not qualified.
- Prospects on discovery calls ask the same question in different words: "so, are you like a [X] tool, or more like a [Y] tool, or something else?" The confusion itself is the signal.
- Pricing conversations are difficult because there is no category price anchor to reference.

**Why the mode is dangerous:** it looks like *innovation*. "We don't fit in any existing category" is the founder's often-proudest claim — and sometimes it is the correct positioning (see below), but usually it is a claim the buyer will not adopt on the founder's schedule.

**Fix:** run Chapter 2 Component 5 correctly. Ask five prospects — after describing the product — *"if you were to look into products like this, what category name would you search for?"* Their words are your candidate labels. Pick one that (a) is a category the buyer already recognises, (b) routes to the right buyer inside the organisation, (c) anchors to a price band you can defend, and (d) is not so crowded you disappear. If none of the buyer-suggested labels fit and you genuinely need a new one, run the [Play Bigger](https://www.playbigger.com/)-style category-design campaign — but budget it as a multi-year, multi-million-dollar effort, not a marketing sprint.

### The one legitimate case for category invention

Not every "we don't fit an existing category" positioning is Mode 3. Occasionally the product genuinely creates a new category, and the invention is defensible. The test:

- The founder has **two years and a Series-B-sized budget** to install the category vocabulary in the buyer's head.
- The company is willing to run a **coordinated multi-channel campaign** — analyst briefings, executive-level thought-leadership, conference sponsorships, dedicated content properties — to install the label.
- The founder can **name a specific incumbent category the new category *replaces*** and articulate why the replacement is inevitable (this is the Chapter 4 strategic-narrative "name the shift" move).
- The **buyer's job title, budget line, and internal decision process** all support a new category (i.e., there is a buyer whose remit already includes the problem the new category solves; they just don't have a name for it yet).

If all four conditions are met, category invention is on the table. Salesforce did this with "cloud CRM," Snowflake with "data cloud," HubSpot with "inbound marketing." Each was a multi-year campaign backed by ample capital. If any of the four conditions is not met, the correct move is *file into an existing category* or *shift to an adjacent one*.

### The diagnostic — running the three-mode check on a product page

The diagnostic is designed to be run *from the outside* — you do not need access to the company's internal positioning document. Product page + a review of published pitch decks + a sales-call transcript or two (or a Gong-style call recording, if publicly available) is enough.

Run in this order — Mode 3 first because category confusion invalidates the others:

```
Q1. Can you name the market category in one phrase the buyer would recognise?
      no  -> MODE 3 (category confusion) fires. Fix Component 5 first.
      yes -> Q2.

Q2. Does the page name specific unique attributes (not comparative adjectives)?
      For every claimed value or "X-er" statement, is there a named attribute
      that produces it?
      no  -> MODE 2 ("we're better") fires. Fix Components 2 and 3.
      yes -> Q3.

Q3. Are the alternatives the page compares against the alternatives the buyer
      actually names when asked what they'd do without the product?
      no  -> MODE 1 (obvious-but-underwhelming) fires. Fix Component 1.
      yes -> Positioning appears defensible. Chapter 4's narrative and Chapter 5's
             beachhead pressure-test remain.
```

Any given product page may fire two or three modes. Fix in the order above — category confusion first, because rewriting attribute → value chains under the wrong category doesn't help; alternatives last, because the alternatives comparison depends on the category and attribute work being done.

### Why "diagnose on real product pages" matters

The exercises in this module (Exercise 02, specifically) require you to run the diagnostic on real, published product pages from real startups. The reason: **your eye for positioning failure gets sharp only when you diagnose companies you don't have empathy for.** Diagnosing your own startup's positioning is difficult because you know the internal narrative and can fill in the gaps the page leaves. A stranger's product page has none of that context — you see only what the page says. That is the reader's condition. Learn to see the page the way the reader does.

The default failure of this exercise is being kind — softening the diagnosis because "well, they probably meant Y." Do not soften. The reader does not fill in gaps for the founder.

## Concrete example — three diagnoses on Loomly

Take the same product (Loomly, PR-review nudge tool) and imagine three product pages the founder might have written:

### Page A — the Mode 1 failure

> **Loomly — the modern alternative to Jellyfish and LinearB. See your engineering bottlenecks in one dashboard, without the six-month rollout.**

Diagnosis: **Mode 1 (obvious-but-underwhelming) fires.** The page positions against Jellyfish and LinearB — expensive enterprise engineering-intelligence platforms that a 30-engineer team is *not* considering. The 30-engineer team is currently doing manual Slack pings, weekly standups, and nothing. The Jellyfish comparison is irrelevant to them; the differentiation is real but pointed at the wrong reference point. A prospect from the 30-engineer segment reads this page, notes that they are not shopping for a Jellyfish alternative, and leaves.

Component 1 fix: interview five to ten mid-market engineering managers with the "what would you do without us" question. The answers converge on "manual Slack pings and eng-manager standups." Rewrite the page against those alternatives. The hero becomes: *"Stop being the reviewer nag. Loomly picks the right reviewer for every stalled PR and pings them so your eng manager doesn't have to."*

### Page B — the Mode 2 failure

> **Loomly — the faster, smarter, easier way to keep your PRs moving. 3× shorter review cycles. 40% happier engineers. Built for modern engineering teams.**

Diagnosis: **Mode 2 ("we're better") fires.** Every claim is a comparative superlative — faster, smarter, easier, 3×, 40%. No unique attribute is named. The buyer has no way to know *why* Loomly produces 3× shorter cycles; without the attribute, the numbers are unsupported. The page reads as marketing noise. The buyer's next question — "okay, but how does it actually work?" — the page does not answer.

Component 2 + 3 fix: name the unique attributes (reviewer-selection logic, load-balancing, escalation ladder) and trace each to a value chain. Hero rewrites to: *"Because Loomly knows who last reviewed similar code and what everyone's current load is, the right reviewer gets nudged — not just anyone. Median review time drops from 3.2 days to 1.1 days in our beta cohort."* The attribute is named; the value is derived; the number is attributed.

### Page C — the Mode 3 failure

> **Loomly — the developer collaboration fabric. AI-native workflow intelligence for the modern engineering platform.**

Diagnosis: **Mode 3 (category confusion) fires.** "Developer collaboration fabric" is a founder invention with no search volume, no analyst coverage, and no buyer-title alignment. "AI-native workflow intelligence" is generic to the point of meaninglessness in 2026. A prospect reading this page cannot tell: are they a chat tool? a project management tool? an observability tool? an IDE plugin? The buyer cannot route the deal to any specific internal owner because no owner recognises the category label as theirs.

Component 5 fix: ask ten engineering managers "if you were looking for a tool to do this, what category would you search for?" Answers converge on "engineering-manager tools," "PR management," "code review automation," "developer productivity." Pick "code review automation" (or "engineering-manager productivity") as the primary label; file the product into the existing shelf. The hero becomes: *"Code review automation for engineering managers who are tired of chasing stale PRs."*

### The stacked case

Real product pages often exhibit two or three modes simultaneously. Here is a plausible stacked-failure Loomly page:

> **Loomly — the developer collaboration fabric. The faster, smarter alternative to Jellyfish and LinearB.**

This page fires all three: category confusion ("collaboration fabric"), we're-better (comparatives without attributes), and obvious-but-underwhelming (comparison against the wrong incumbents). Fix in Chapter 3's diagnostic order: pick a real category label first (Mode 3), then trace attribute-value chains (Mode 2), then re-select the alternatives against the real buyer (Mode 1). The order matters — the attribute-value chains you write depend on which category and which alternatives you are positioning against.

## Common failure patterns

- **Being kind to the diagnosis.** "Well, they probably meant Y" is the death of the exercise. The buyer does not fill in Y. Diagnose the page as written.
- **Diagnosing your own page first.** You know the internal narrative; you cannot see your own page cold. Start with stranger companies until the eye is sharp, then turn it on your own.
- **Naming Mode 1 by default whenever the page underperforms.** All three modes underperform. Alternatives-vs-category-vs-attribute is a distinct diagnostic; run all three questions.
- **Treating "we're a new category" as a defensible Mode 3 escape.** Category invention is legitimate under specific conditions (multi-year budget, coordinated campaign, aligned buyer title). Most startups do not meet the conditions.
- **Fixing Mode 2 without fixing Mode 3.** If the category is wrong, the attribute → value chains you write will point at the wrong buyer. Diagnose Mode 3 first.
- **Rewriting the hero without touching the positioning.** The hero is the last thing you rewrite, not the first. Chapter 6 (messaging derivation) is downstream of the positioning fix; do not skip the fix and go straight to words.
- **Confusing "clever wording" with fixed positioning.** A cleverly written hero on top of Mode 1 positioning still under-delivers because it is pointed at the wrong reference point. The words are downstream.

## Summary

- Three failure modes at the resolution the Chapter 2 frame can distinguish: **obvious-but-underwhelming (Mode 1), "we're better" (Mode 2), category confusion (Mode 3).**
- Each maps to a specific broken component: **Mode 1 → Component 1 (alternatives); Mode 2 → Components 2 and 3 (attributes and value); Mode 3 → Component 5 (category).**
- The diagnostic runs in **reverse order** — Mode 3 → Mode 2 → Mode 1 — because category selection constrains attribute/value work, which in turn constrains alternatives comparison.
- The three modes **often stack**. A single page can exhibit all three. Diagnose them separately even when they co-occur.
- Category invention is legitimate only under **four conditions** — long runway, coordinated campaign, replaceable incumbent category, aligned buyer title. Most startups do not meet the bar.
- Diagnose on **real product pages** to sharpen the eye. Your own page is the hardest to see clearly because you fill in the gaps unconsciously.
- The mode determines the remedy. **Name the mode before rewriting the words** — otherwise you are on the third tagline rewrite with nothing moving.
