# Exercise 02 — Diagnose the Three Positioning Failure Modes on Real Product Pages

**Estimated time:** 3 hours
**Chapter link:** [`03-three-positioning-failure-modes.md`](../03-three-positioning-failure-modes.md)
**Prerequisite:** Chapter 2 (Dunford five components) understood; Chapter 3 (failure modes) read end-to-end

## Problem statement

Chapter 3 named three positioning failure modes — **obvious-but-underwhelming (Mode 1)**, **"we're better" (Mode 2)**, and **category confusion (Mode 3)** — each with a specific diagnostic signature and a specific broken component. This exercise trains you to run the diagnostic *cold* on real product pages you did not author, and to prescribe the specific Chapter 2 component-level fix per failure.

The single reason to diagnose from the outside — on strangers' product pages — is that your eye for positioning failure only becomes sharp when you cannot fill in the founder's internal narrative. On your own page, you know what you *meant*; the reader does not. The exercise is designed to make you see pages the way the reader sees them.

## Requirements

Deliver a folder `exercise-02/` with:

- `part-a-provided-diagnoses.md` — diagnoses of two provided product-page mock-ups.
- `part-b-live-diagnoses.md` — diagnoses of three real, live product pages you find.
- `part-c-my-own-diagnosis.md` — a diagnosis of your own startup's product page (or, if pre-launch, your current pitch deck's cover slide + one-sentence description).

### Part A — Diagnose two provided product-page mock-ups (45 min)

The following are two fictionalised but realistic startup product-page mock-ups. Diagnose each.

#### Mock-up 1

> **Chronos — the modern alternative to Confluence, Notion, and SharePoint.**
> *Faster, smarter, more collaborative. Built for modern knowledge teams. See why 500+ companies have switched.*
> *[Get a demo] [Compare us to Confluence]*

For this mock-up, produce:

1. **The five-component read of what the page positions.** Fill in Chapter 2's five components based *only* on the text of the page — no charitable interpretation.
2. **The Chapter 3 diagnostic tree walk.** Q1 (category) → Q2 (attributes) → Q3 (alternatives). Show your work at each step.
3. **Verdict.** Which modes fire? If more than one, name each.
4. **The specific component-level fix.** Per Chapter 3's failure-mode → component map: which Chapter 2 component would you rebuild first, and why.
5. **A rewritten hero (one paragraph)** — a version that would fix the diagnosed mode(s). Do not solve for all possible modes; solve for the one you diagnosed.

#### Mock-up 2

> **Nucleus — the AI-native workflow intelligence platform.**
> *Nucleus is the operating system for modern go-to-market teams. Powered by AI. Built for the future of work.*
> *[Learn more]*

Same five deliverables as Mock-up 1.

The two mock-ups are engineered to fire different mode combinations. Mock-up 1 is designed to fire Mode 1 (obvious-but-underwhelming, comparing to enterprise incumbents the buyer isn't shopping for) *and* Mode 2 (comparative superlatives without traceable attributes). Mock-up 2 is designed to fire Mode 3 (category confusion — "AI-native workflow intelligence platform" is category invention with generic labels) and possibly Mode 2. The exercise is not to guess the intended diagnosis; the exercise is to *walk the tree* and show the diagnostic reasoning.

### Part B — Diagnose three real, live product pages (75 min)

Find **three real, live product pages** — startups whose sites are actually online today. Sources:

- Recent Y Combinator batch launches — the [YC company directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies) lists current-batch companies with linked product pages.
- Product Hunt "Featured" launches from the last 12 months.
- Startups covered on [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/) or [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/) with a positioning-relevant angle.
- Startups you have personally considered buying from or interviewed with.

Pick a mix. Ideally:
- One that you think fires Mode 1 (obvious-but-underwhelming).
- One that you think fires Mode 2 ("we're better").
- One that you think fires Mode 3 (category confusion).

If you cannot find three unambiguous examples, pick three that fire *any* mode(s) and diagnose what you see. Do not pick three pages that are all clean — the diagnostic muscle develops on messy pages.

For each of the three, produce a diagnosis in the same shape as Part A:

1. **Company name + URL + date accessed.**
2. **The five-component read.** Fill Chapter 2's five components based only on the page content. Include a `Not stated` line for any component the page does not articulate.
3. **The diagnostic tree walk** — Q1 → Q2 → Q3.
4. **Verdict** — modes that fire.
5. **The component-level fix.**
6. **Rewritten hero** — one paragraph per page.

For each verdict, include one sentence naming **the specific signal on the page** that fired the diagnosis. "The hero copy 'the modern alternative to Confluence' names an enterprise incumbent that SMB buyers wouldn't be shopping for" is a specific signal; "the page feels off" is not.

### Part C — Diagnose your own startup's page (60 min)

Repeat the exercise on your own startup's product page. If pre-launch, use the pitch-deck cover slide + one-sentence description in the corner of the deck.

Same six-deliverable shape as Part B, plus one addition:

7. **Reflection paragraph** — one paragraph naming which mode(s) you fired and, honestly, which Chapter 2 component of your own positioning you would rebuild in response. If no mode fires, one paragraph naming why the page is defensible (with the specific signals that support "defensible").

The addition exists because the whole point of diagnosing your own page is what you *do* with the diagnosis. A team that runs the diagnostic on themselves and takes no action has wasted the exercise.

## Starter guidance

- Read Chapter 3 end-to-end before starting. The three-mode diagnostic tree is the operating instrument.
- The diagnostic runs in **reverse order**: Mode 3 first (category confusion — because it invalidates the others), then Mode 2 (attributes / value), then Mode 1 (alternatives). Follow the order.
- Diagnose from the *page as written*, not from what you imagine the founder meant. The reader does not fill in gaps.
- For Part B, look at the *hero above the fold* and the *first section below the fold* — that is what a visitor sees in the first 20 seconds and where the diagnosis lives. Do not go deep into product-tour content.
- Mode 2 ("we're better") is the most common; roughly half of live SaaS product pages exhibit some Mode 2 pattern. Do not default-diagnose everything as Mode 2; if the mode is present, it should be visible in specific comparative-adjective language on the page.
- Mode 3 (category confusion) is the most fun to diagnose because the invented category labels are usually vivid. But watch for the *legitimate* category-invention cases — occasionally the founder has invested in a coordinated multi-year campaign to install the vocabulary and the invention is defensible. Chapter 3 names the four-conditions test.
- The **rewritten hero** in each deliverable is the point of the exercise. Naming a failure mode without prescribing the fix is dismissal, not diagnosis. The rewrite forces you to derive from the (corrected) positioning.
- For Part C, do not be kind to yourself. If your own hero fires Mode 2, name it and rewrite it. The whole reason the exercise ends with self-diagnosis is to catch the pattern in your own work.
- If your own page fires all three modes stacked, that is *expected* for early-stage — most pre-Series-A product pages have not been through the discipline. The exercise output is *not* a failing grade; it is a punch list.

## Acceptance criteria

Your submission is complete when:

- [ ] Part A diagnoses both provided mock-ups with all five deliverables (five-component read, diagnostic tree walk, verdict, component-level fix, rewritten hero).
- [ ] The diagnostic tree walk shows the actual Q1 → Q2 → Q3 branches — not a summary verdict without work shown.
- [ ] Part B diagnoses three real, live product pages (with URLs and dates) with the same six-deliverable shape. Each verdict names the *specific* signal on the page that fired it.
- [ ] The three pages in Part B span different modes where possible. If all three fire the same mode, one paragraph in the submission explains why the sample skewed (and, ideally, adds a fourth page to compensate).
- [ ] Part C diagnoses your own page (or pitch-deck cover) with the six deliverables + the reflection paragraph naming which component you would rebuild.
- [ ] No verdict is unstated. "The page has some issues" is not a verdict; "Mode 2 fires because the hero says 'the smartest CRM' without naming any structural attribute that produces the 'smart'" is a verdict.
- [ ] Every rewritten hero is written in the derived-from-positioning shape (Chapter 6), not decorative language.
- [ ] For any live-page diagnosis where you cannot infer the true underlying positioning (because you don't have access to the founder's internal doc), note the ambiguity explicitly rather than guessing.

## Common ways this exercise goes wrong

- **Being kind.** "Well, they probably meant Y" is the death of the exercise. Diagnose the page as written; the reader does not have Y available.
- **Diagnosing from the "About" page or the docs.** The failure mode lives in the hero. Diagnose above the fold. Founders often have a coherent positioning in an internal doc that never made it to the marketing page — the exercise is what the buyer sees, not what the founder knows.
- **Naming Mode 1 whenever the page underperforms.** All three modes underperform. If you never diagnose Mode 2 or Mode 3, you are skipping the tree.
- **Diagnosing without a rewrite.** The rewrite is where the exercise pays off. It forces you to derive the corrected messaging from the corrected positioning.
- **Mode 3 diagnosis without checking the four-conditions test.** Occasionally the "invented category" is defensible because the company is running the multi-year campaign. Check.
- **Being kind to your own page.** Part C exists exactly because self-diagnosis is the hardest and most valuable. Say the ugly thing on the page.
- **Sampling only failing pages.** Include at least one page in Part B that is defensible — it sharpens the diagnostic to know what "no mode fires" looks like on a real page.
- **Confusing "clever wording" with fixed positioning.** A rewritten hero that is witty but still on top of broken positioning does not count. The rewrite must derive from a *corrected* Chapter 2 component.
