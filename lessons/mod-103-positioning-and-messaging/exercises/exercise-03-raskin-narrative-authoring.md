# Exercise 03 — Raskin Strategic-Narrative Authoring

**Estimated time:** 4 hours
**Chapter links:** [`04-raskin-strategic-narrative.md`](../04-raskin-strategic-narrative.md), [`02-dunford-five-components.md`](../02-dunford-five-components.md)
**Depends on:** Exercise 01 (Dunford five-component drill) — the positioning it produces is the raw material this narrative wraps around.
**Prerequisite reading:** [Andy Raskin — "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (2016)](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0) end-to-end (~20 min); Chapter 4 (~30 min).

## Problem statement

Chapter 4 named the six-part strategic-narrative shape — **shift → enemy → promised land → evidence → obstacles → tools** — and argued that a narrative structured this way is more resilient than a product-feature deck because (a) it places the world's change at the top instead of the product, (b) it names an outmoded worldview as the enemy (not a competitor), and (c) it serves customer / AE-hire / investor audiences off the same six-part backbone.

This exercise makes you author the six parts in full, then draft the ten-to-fifteen-slide deck, then produce three audience-appropriate closes (one for a first customer, one for a first AE candidate, one for a seed investor). At the end you will have a shippable strategic-narrative artifact — the second of the three artifacts the module ships in Chapter 7.

You are authoring the narrative for **the same startup you positioned in Exercise 01**. The Dunford five-component positioning is the raw material; this exercise wraps that positioning in the persuasive arc that turns "here is our positioning" into "here is why the world in which our product is inevitable is happening right now."

## Requirements

Deliver a folder `exercise-03/` with:

- `part-a-six-part-authoring.md` — the six parts as prose (one section per part).
- `part-b-deck-structure.md` — the 10-15-slide deck outline (slide-by-slide, with the messaging beat each slide carries).
- `part-c-audience-closes.md` — the three audience-appropriate closing slide sets.
- Optionally, `deck.pdf` / `deck.pptx` / a link to a Google Slides deck if you want to render slides. The written outline in Part B is the required deliverable; rendered slides are optional.

### Part A — Author the six parts as prose (90 min)

For each of the six parts, write one section (200-400 words). Each section should stand alone as a persuasive argument that the previous section's claim leads inevitably to this one. The reader who has finished Part A should agree with the six-step chain even without slides.

Use the following template — one heading per part. Do not skip parts; do not merge them.

#### 1. The shift (what changed in the world?)

- **The one-sentence claim.** Name the specific change in the world in one sentence. Not "AI is happening" — a specific shift. Chapter 4 lists the four categories that usually qualify (regulatory, technology capability, buyer-behaviour, structural cost).
- **The evidence.** Cite one primary source that documents the shift (research paper, regulatory filing, market-size delta, cost curve, peer-reviewed analysis). If you cannot cite a source, mark it `<!-- needs-research: [what you need] -->` and continue — but the deck will not ship without the citation.
- **Why now, not five years ago and not five years from now.** One paragraph naming what specifically became true recently that makes this the *moment*. A shift that has been true for a decade is not a shift.

#### 2. The enemy (who loses the shift?)

- **Name the outmoded worldview** — not a competitor company. Chapter 4's Zuora example named "the product economy" as the enemy of "the subscription economy." Your enemy is a *way of working* or a *mental model* that no longer fits.
- **Show one or two ways the enemy's model breaks under the shift.** Concrete — a workflow that doesn't scale, an assumption that no longer holds, a role whose responsibilities have shifted.
- **Do not straw-man.** Buyers know the enemy; if you describe it dishonestly, they disengage. Name the enemy's genuine past strength before showing why the shift breaks it.

#### 3. The promised land (what does the winners' world look like?)

- **Paint the concrete state** — what the buyer's business, day, or customer looks like when they have made the transition. Not "your business runs better." Chapter 4's example: "finance closes the books in three days, not three weeks; the CFO trusts the numbers; the audit committee stops asking for reconciliation memos."
- **Use the buyer's language, not the founder's.** Ideally an outcome the buyer has personally wanted for years.
- **Do not mention your product yet.** The promised land is the *destination*; your product is the vehicle (Part 6). If your product goes away tomorrow, the promised land is still what the buyer wants.

#### 4. The evidence (why should the audience believe?)

- **Cite ≥ 3 pieces of evidence** that the shift is real and the promised land is reachable. Chapter 4 lists what qualifies: published statistics with sources, public cases of early winners, third-party trend charts, voice-of-customer quotes.
- **Every stat has a source.** No fabricated or approximated numbers. Uncited numbers erode the whole deck's credibility. If you don't have a source, `<!-- needs-research -->` it.
- **Name at least one early-winner case** — a company (or a class of companies) that has already crossed over. Ideally credible to the audience (household name, known brand in their industry, or audience-adjacent competitor).

#### 5. The obstacles (what stands between the audience and the promised land?)

- **Name 3-5 specific, felt obstacles.** Technical, organisational, budgetary, competency-based — pick the ones the audience will nod at.
- **Not "change is hard."** Generic obstacles are decoration. Each obstacle should be specific enough that the audience recognises it from their own experience.
- **Sequence them.** The obstacles should motivate the tools in Part 6 — each obstacle should have a tool that answers it. If an obstacle has no tool, either the obstacle is decoration or the tools list is incomplete.

#### 6. The tools (what tools — including yours — clear the obstacles?)

- **Map each tool to a Part 5 obstacle.** Numbered response — obstacle 1 is answered by tool 1, obstacle 2 by tool 2, etc.
- **Your product is not the *only* tool.** Name the other things the audience will use too (a data warehouse, an existing CRM, a partner ecosystem, human expertise). This makes the pitch feel realistic and situates your product in the buyer's real world.
- **Frame your product at the level of the buyer's outcome, not the feature list.** "Our product handles X so that Y" — not "our product has features A, B, C, D." The features go on the product page (Exercise 06); the strategic narrative operates at outcome altitude.

### Part B — 10-15-slide deck outline (60 min)

Deliver `part-b-deck-structure.md` with the slide-by-slide outline. Each row in the table below is a slide.

| # | Slide title (draft copy) | Six-part role | Body (one line: chart / image / claim / bullets) | Speaker notes (2-3 sentences) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title slide | — | Startup name + one-line description + date | — |
| 2 | [Slide title from your Part A shift] | Shift | Chart / image / one-sentence claim citing your Part 4 evidence source | Notes on how you'd talk to this slide |
| 3 | [Slide title from your Part A enemy] | Enemy | "Then vs. now" contrast or the enemy's model breaking | Notes |
| 4 | [Slide title from your Part A promised land] | Promised land | Concrete signals of the promised state (list, image, or narrative) | Notes |
| 5 | [Evidence slide 1] | Evidence | Cited statistic or chart | Notes with source link |
| 6 | [Evidence slide 2 — early-winner case] | Evidence | Named company / class of companies + one concrete result | Notes with source link |
| 7 | [Obstacle 1] | Obstacles | One obstacle, well-explained | Notes |
| 8 | [Obstacle 2] | Obstacles | Second obstacle | Notes |
| 9 | [Obstacle 3 or 4] | Obstacles | Additional obstacles (combine if you have 4-5 short ones) | Notes |
| 10 | [Tools slide — your product's role] | Tools | Each obstacle → the tool that clears it; your product's specific role named at outcome altitude | Notes |
| 11-15 | Audience-specific close (see Part C) | Close | — | — |

**Deck hygiene requirements (mirror Chapter 7):**

- **The product does not appear before slide ~8-10.** If the founder's logo, product name, or feature list is on slides 1-7, the deck has collapsed back into a product-feature shape. Move it.
- **Every claim on every slide is sourced.** Charts have citations; statistics link to the source; case studies name the customer. Unsourced claims — even in the shift or evidence slides — poison the deck.
- **One idea per slide.** Not four bullet points; one claim, well-supported. If a slide has four ideas, break it into four slides or cut three of the ideas.
- **Consistent voice.** The deck's language matches the positioning-document language matches the product-page language. If your positioning says "engineering managers" and your deck says "eng leads" and the product page says "team leads," the reader notices and the company loses coherence.

### Part C — Three audience-appropriate closes (45 min)

The core 10 slides are the same for all three audiences; only the last 2-3 slides differ. Author all three closes in `part-c-audience-closes.md`.

#### C1 — Customer close (a first paying customer)

Two to three slides that translate the strategic narrative into a next-step for the buyer:

- **Slide N+1: Next step.** Concrete — a demo, a trial signup, a design-partner conversation, a pilot scope. Not "learn more."
- **Slide N+2: Deal shape (optional).** If the sales motion has a defined pilot shape (30-day, N-user, $X price), state it.
- **Speaker notes:** how you would deliver the close conversationally — the question you would ask, the "what's the smallest experiment we could run together" framing.

#### C2 — AE-candidate close (a first sales-hire interview)

Two to three slides that let a prospective AE evaluate whether they can sell this:

- **Slide N+1: The sales-cycle arc.** Cold outreach → discovery call → demo → pilot → close, with typical duration per stage. Chapter 4 argues this is what a first AE reads the deck for — the "can I imagine running this?" test.
- **Slide N+2: Deal shape + comp.** ACV band, expected close rate, quota shape, OTE. Enough for the AE to reason about whether the deal shape supports the comp.
- **Slide N+3 (optional): Team + why-now for the hire.** Who's on the sales team today; what the AE would own; the specific gap they'd fill.
- **Speaker notes:** the objection-handling — the two or three "yes, but…" questions the AE will ask (how do you compete against X? what's the win rate against status quo? how are pilots priced?) and one-paragraph answers.

#### C3 — Seed-investor close

Two to three slides that translate the strategic narrative into a fundraising ask:

- **Slide N+1: Market sizing + traction.** The Part 1 shift + the Part 4 evidence, sized. Named early customers (if any) + ACV + retention read (if any). This is the investor's "is the shift real, is this team capturing it" test.
- **Slide N+2: Raise ask + use of funds.** Round size, valuation range (optional), the specific hires / product bets / market experiments the raise funds. Chapter 4's investor audience wants to know whether this team is the winner-take-most bet.
- **Slide N+3 (optional): Team.** Why *this* team is the right one to capture the shift. Prior operating experience relevant to the shift; not résumés.
- **Speaker notes:** the investor's likely objections — "why now, why you, why won't [incumbent] fold this in" — and one-paragraph answers.

### (Optional) Render the deck

If you want to render actual slides, use whatever tool you're comfortable with (Google Slides, Keynote, PowerPoint, Pitch, Deck.rs, Marp). Rendered slides are optional; the Part B outline is the required deliverable. If you do render, the design-quality bar for this exercise is *legible*, not *polished* — do not spend more than 30 minutes on design.

## Starter guidance

- Read the [Raskin essay](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0) end-to-end before starting. The essay's Zuora example is the shape you're mirroring; the essay is short enough to read twice.
- **The single most common mistake is opening with the product.** If your slide 2 has your product name on it, you are writing a product-feature deck with a "shift" fig-leaf. Move the product to slide ~10 and rewrite slide 2 as a shift the audience can accept without knowing your product exists.
- **The enemy is a worldview, not a competitor.** Naming Salesforce as the enemy collapses the deck into a competitive-pitch shape. Name what Salesforce *represents* — "the CRM-as-source-of-truth assumption when the rep never opens the CRM" — as the enemy instead.
- **The promised land is the buyer's language, not yours.** If you find yourself writing about your product's capabilities in Part 3, delete that sentence and rewrite it as an outcome. "Your engineering manager gets their afternoon back" is promised land; "our reviewer-selection engine matches CODEOWNERS with prior review history" is not.
- **Evidence is where credibility lives or dies.** One uncited number in the deck poisons the audience's trust in every other claim. If you cannot cite a source for a statistic, mark it `<!-- needs-research -->` and either find the source or drop the statistic before shipping.
- **Obstacles set up tools.** Every obstacle in Part 5 should map to a tool in Part 6. If Part 5 has five obstacles and Part 6 has three tools, either two obstacles have no answer (the pitch has holes) or Part 5 has decoration you should cut.
- **The three audience closes share the six-part backbone.** Do not rewrite Parts 1-6 for each audience — the whole point of the shape is that one narrative serves all three. Only the last 2-3 slides change.
- If you're at hour 3 and stuck on Part A, cut. A shipped rough draft is more valuable than a polished half-draft. You will iterate.
- **The narrative depends on the positioning.** If you find that Part 3's promised land contradicts your Exercise 01 Component 3 (value), one of the two is wrong. Reconcile — the positioning is the source of truth; adjust the narrative to match, or (if the narrative surfaced a better value claim) update the positioning and note the version bump.

## Acceptance criteria

Your submission is complete when:

- [ ] Part A has all six parts, each 200-400 words, each standing alone as a persuasive argument.
- [ ] Part 1 (shift) names a specific change with a cited primary source (or a `needs-research` marker for the source).
- [ ] Part 2 (enemy) names a worldview, not a competitor company. If a competitor name appears in Part 2, rewrite.
- [ ] Part 3 (promised land) uses the buyer's language, names ≥ 3 concrete signals of the promised state, and does not mention your product by name.
- [ ] Part 4 (evidence) has ≥ 3 pieces of cited evidence — statistics with sources, an early-winner case, or a third-party trend chart. Every stat has a source or a `needs-research` marker.
- [ ] Part 5 (obstacles) names 3-5 specific, felt obstacles. No "change is hard."
- [ ] Part 6 (tools) maps each obstacle to a tool, names your product at outcome altitude (not feature list), and names ≥ 1 non-your-product tool the audience will also use.
- [ ] Part B has a 10-15-slide outline with the six-part role labelled on each slide, per-slide body, and speaker notes.
- [ ] Your product does not appear on any slide before the tools slide (~8-10).
- [ ] Every stat in the deck has a source or a `needs-research` marker.
- [ ] Part C has three audience-appropriate closes (customer / AE / investor), each with 2-3 additional slides and speaker notes with objection-handling.
- [ ] The narrative's Part 3 (promised land) and Part 6 (tools) are consistent with the Exercise 01 Dunford Component 3 (value) and Component 5 (category). Any contradictions are reconciled explicitly.

## Common ways this exercise goes wrong

- **Opening with the product / logo / team.** The whole Chapter 4 point. Move the product to slide ~10.
- **Enemy = named competitor.** Collapses the deck into a competitive-pitch shape. Enemy is a worldview.
- **Promised land = product features.** Delete and rewrite as outcomes. The promised land exists whether or not your product does.
- **Uncited evidence.** One uncited number poisons the deck. If you can't source it, mark it or cut it.
- **Generic obstacles.** "Change is hard" — cut. Every obstacle should be specific enough that the audience recognises it.
- **Tools slide as feature list.** Frame each tool as the answer to a Part 5 obstacle; name your product at outcome altitude, not feature altitude.
- **Three separate decks for three audiences.** The whole discipline is one backbone; only the closes differ. If you find yourself rewriting Parts 1-6 for the investor version, the positioning is unstable and the narrative won't hold up.
- **Founder monologue.** The narrative should read as *the audience's* argument, not the founder's opinion. Sentences that begin with "we believe" are usually the founder's opinion; find the version that starts with the buyer's experience.
- **Skipping the audience-specific closes.** The closes are what turn the narrative from a deck into a next-step. Without them the audience finishes the deck agreeing with you and then does nothing.
- **Over-designing the slides.** Design polish is not what this exercise is graded on. A rough outline that reasons cleanly beats a polished deck with holes in the argument.
