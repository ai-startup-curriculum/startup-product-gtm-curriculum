# Shipping the Positioning Artifact — One-Page Doc + Narrative Deck + Revised Headline

## Motivation

Six chapters of alternatives, attributes, value chains, failure-mode diagnostics, six-part narratives, and beachhead selection produce a lot of paper. **The deliverable is not the paper.** The deliverable is a small, tightly-bounded artifact set that a founder, a co-founder, a first hire, an investor, or the founder-two-weeks-from-now can pick up and act on in one sitting.

Three artifacts, each with a specific reader and a specific decision it enables:

1. A **one-page positioning document** — the internal operating brief that names the five components, the beachhead, and the not-now list. Read by the founding team, product, and sales. Not shown to customers.
2. A **10-15-slide strategic-narrative deck** — the six-part Raskin-shape story that translates the positioning into a persuasive arc across three audiences. Read by first customers (in modified form), first AE hires, and first investors.
3. A **revised product-page headline + subhead + hero copy + CTA — three variants** — the derivation of the positioning into buyer-facing words on the highest-traffic surface. Instrumented and A/B-tested.

All three are outputs of the earlier chapters. All three are versioned, dated, and signed. The module's shipping discipline treats them as the *ship deliverable* — everything upstream is raw material.

This chapter names the shape, the length, and the evidence bar for each artifact, and shows how the artifact set feeds forward into the rest of the track.

## Core concepts

### Artifact 1 — the one-page positioning document

The positioning document is the internal operating brief. It is *not* marketing copy; it uses whatever vocabulary is clearest (technical, industry-specific, jargon-heavy if useful). It exists to give the founding team, product, sales, and any new hire a single source of truth for *which market context the product wins in*.

**Sections (six, non-negotiable, in order):**

1. **Header** — startup name, date, author, version number, and prior versions with dates. Positioning has a shelf life; the header is what tells the reader whether it is still current.
2. **Competitive alternatives** — the actual alternatives the buyer names when asked "what would you do without us?" (Chapter 2 Component 1). Not the direct-competitor list. Sourced from buyer interviews or the mod-102 very-disappointed free-text.
3. **Unique attributes** — the 3-5 properties you have that the *named alternatives* lack (Chapter 2 Component 2). Each with a sentence naming which alternative it is unique against.
4. **Value** — the buyer-consequence of each attribute, traced as attribute → value → buyer-consequence, one line each (Chapter 2 Component 3). Every value claim traces to a named attribute.
5. **Best-for characteristics + beachhead segment + not-now list** — the best-for characteristics (Chapter 2 Component 4), the specific beachhead slice picked from within the best-for space (Chapter 5), and the explicit list of segments being said no to for now, each with a reason (Chapter 5's "not-now" discipline).
6. **Market category + adjacent-category considered + not-now** — the primary category (Chapter 2 Component 5), any adjacent category considered but not chosen, and one line on why. If category invention was considered, one line on why it was or wasn't chosen and what the campaign would cost.

**Length and tone constraints:**

- **One page — two at maximum.** If it does not fit, cut. The positioning document is a working brief, not a strategy consultancy deliverable.
- **Opinionated, not neutral.** Every component is a *decision*. "Our primary category is X, not Y, because [reason]" is a decision; "We are considering X and Y as categories" is a memo, not a positioning document.
- **Sourced.** Every alternative names the buyer interview or free-text quote it came from. Every unique attribute names the product surface that delivers it. Every value claim traces the attribute. Every best-for characteristic has a one-sentence reason.
- **Dated, signed, versioned.** Same discipline as the mod-101 discovery report and the mod-102 PMF scorecard. Positioning changes rarely, but when it does, the delta between versions is the record of the strategic shift.

### The one-page positioning document template

A minimal template. Keep to this shape; add nothing.

```
POSITIONING DOCUMENT — [Startup name]
Author: [Founder / CEO]. Version [N]. Date: [YYYY-MM-DD].
Prior versions: [v1: 2025-11-15 — pre-beachhead pivot], [v2: 2026-02-01 — post-mid-market wins]

1. COMPETITIVE ALTERNATIVES
   The alternatives buyers actually name when asked "what would you do without us?":
   - [Alternative 1] — cited by [N] of [M] interviewed buyers; [context]
   - [Alternative 2] — cited by [N] of [M] interviewed buyers; [context]
   - [Alternative 3] — cited by [N] of [M] interviewed buyers; [context]
   Notably NOT the alternative: [direct competitor X, per buyer interviews]

2. UNIQUE ATTRIBUTES
   Properties we have that the named alternatives lack:
   - [Attribute 1] — unique against [alternative X and Y]; delivered by [product surface / feature]
   - [Attribute 2] — unique against [alternative Z]; delivered by [product surface]
   - [Attribute 3] — unique against [all named alternatives]; delivered by [product surface]

3. VALUE (traced from attributes)
   - [Attribute 1] → [buyer-consequence, ideally with a number]
   - [Attribute 2] → [buyer-consequence]
   - [Attribute 3] → [buyer-consequence]

4. BEST-FOR CHARACTERISTICS + BEACHHEAD + NOT NOW
   Best-for characteristics (customer traits that predict high value):
   - [Firmographic characteristic] — because [one-sentence reason]
   - [Situational / behavioural characteristic] — because [reason]
   - [Technical characteristic] — because [reason]
   - [Additional characteristic] — because [reason]

   Beachhead segment (first slice we are committing to, per Chapter 5):
   [Segment definition — narrower than the best-for; six-criteria defended.]

   Not-now segments (segments we are saying no to for the next 12 months):
   - [Segment A] — reason: [whole-product gap / not reference-able / adjacent expansion doesn't carry / etc.]
   - [Segment B] — reason: [reason]

5. MARKET CATEGORY
   Primary category: [Category name] — the shelf buyers use to search / route decisions.
   Adjacent category considered: [Alternative category] — not chosen because [reason].
   Category invention considered? [Yes / No, with one-line reason]

6. POSITIONING SUMMARY (one paragraph — the internal-brief version)
   For [beachhead segment] who [top job / pain], [product] is a [category] that [top-value].
   Unlike [named alternative], we [key differentiating attribute].

APPENDIX (linked)
   - Buyer interviews sourcing the alternatives set: [link]
   - Product surfaces delivering the attributes: [link]
   - The mod-101 discovery corpus and mod-102 PMF scorecard the positioning derives from: [links]
```

That is the whole document. One page filled cleanly is worth more than any 30-page positioning-agency deck. The Moore compressed summary at section 6 is the *elevator-pitch* derivation from the earlier sections — not the input, the output.

### Artifact 2 — the strategic-narrative deck

The Chapter 4 six-part story turned into a specific deck. This chapter does not re-teach the six parts; it names the *deck-shipping* discipline that separates a working deck from a working narrative.

**Deck length and shape:**
- **10-15 slides.** Fewer is often better. Every slide earns its place; if a slide is filler, cut it.
- **Six-part backbone.** Every deck includes: the shift, the enemy, the promised land, the evidence, the obstacles, the tools. Missing a part breaks the sequence.
- **Audience-appropriate closing slides.** The core 10-12 slides are the same for customer, AE candidate, and investor. The last 2-3 slides are audience-specific — a customer deck ends with "next steps" (a demo, a trial signup); an AE candidate deck ends with the deal-shape and comp; an investor deck ends with the raise ask and use of funds. Same backbone; audience-appropriate close.

**Deck-hygiene rules:**
- **Every claim on every slide is sourced.** Charts have citations; statistics have source links; case studies name the customer. Unsourced claims — even in the shift or evidence slides — poison the deck.
- **No product on slides 1-6.** The product does not appear until the tools slide (slide 8-10 typically). This is the whole point of the Raskin shape; a product-first opener collapses back into a product-feature deck.
- **One idea per slide.** Not four bullet points; one claim, well-supported.
- **Consistent voice.** The deck's language matches the positioning-document language matches the product-page language. If the deck says "engineering managers" and the positioning says "engineering leads" and the product page says "team leads," the reader notices; the company loses coherence.

**Version-control discipline:**
- The deck is a living document; ship the current version with a date on the last slide.
- Prior versions are archived, not deleted. The deltas between versions are the record of narrative evolution.
- The founder — not the marketing agency — owns the deck. Positioning and narrative are strategic decisions; delegating them to an agency produces polished but non-committal decks.

### Artifact 3 — the revised product-page headline (three variants)

The Chapter 6 derivation, shipped. This is the smallest of the three artifacts but the highest-instrumented.

**Deliverable shape (per Chapter 6):**
- **Three variants** — always three, never one. Each with headline + subhead + primary CTA. Each variant leads on a different Chapter 2 component (usually one on value, one on attribute, one on enemy-of-status-quo).
- **Each variant traces to the positioning document.** Which component is leading; which alternative it distinguishes against; which best-for characteristic it filters for.
- **Ship-and-test.** A/B test the three variants against a defined success metric (demo-request rate, trial-signup rate, click-through to secondary content — whichever is the operating funnel metric).

**What the artifact contains:**
- The three headline + subhead + CTA variants, each with the component-lead noted.
- The A/B test plan — sample size target, minimum-detectable-effect, success metric, test duration.
- The baseline current-hero performance (if any) as a reference point.
- A commitment to *not* interpret the test result as evidence of positioning failure — a losing headline variant is a losing headline variant, not a broken positioning. Three consecutive rounds of all-losing variants triggers the Chapter 3 failure-mode diagnostic.

### Why three artifacts, not one

A common founder impulse is to combine all three into one document — "positioning deck," "our story," "brand book." Do not. The three artifacts have three different readers and three different decisions:

| Artifact | Reader | Decision it enables |
|---|---|---|
| One-page positioning document | Founding team, product, first sales hire, first PMM hire | *"Which market context do we win in, and what does 'we do X, not Y' commit us to?"* — the internal operating brief |
| 10-15-slide strategic-narrative deck | First customers, first sales hires, first investors | *"Do I believe the shift is real, the promised land reachable, and this team's tools the right ones to get there?"* — the persuasive arc |
| 3-variant revised product-page hero | Anonymous inbound traffic on the product page | *"Should I keep reading, or leave?"* — the surface-level conversion decision |

Combining them collapses the readers and dilutes each decision. Keep them separate; version them separately.

### How the artifact set feeds forward

The three artifacts are the input to five downstream modules and two projects of this track:

- **[mod-104 (ICP and Buyer Personas)](../mod-104-icp-and-buyer-personas)** takes the beachhead segment and best-for characteristics as the seed of the scoreable ICP + disqualification checklist. An ICP contradicting the positioning is broken.
- **[mod-105 (Founder-Led Sales)](../mod-105-founder-led-sales)** takes the narrative deck as the founder's sales pitch and derives the discovery-call script, demo structure, and objection-handling from the positioning's value chains.
- **[mod-106 (Pricing and Packaging)](../mod-106-pricing-and-packaging)** takes the market category (Component 5) as the primary price anchor and the beachhead segment's willingness-to-pay as the primary pricing input. Pricing that contradicts the category's price band collides with buyer expectation.
- **[mod-107 (Sales Motion Design)](../mod-107-sales-motion-design)** takes the best-for characteristics + beachhead + category as the ICP-and-ACV inputs that determine motion (PLG / SDR-AE / enterprise-MEDDPICC). The motion follows the beachhead, not the founder's preference.
- **[mod-108 (Demand Generation and Channels)](../mod-108-demand-generation-and-channels)** takes the beachhead segment's peer-community locations (from the Chapter 5 reference-able criterion) as the primary channel candidates. Channel-market fit is beachhead-specific.
- **[`project-101-first-thirty-paying-customers`](../../projects/project-101-first-thirty-paying-customers)** takes the beachhead + narrative as the primary sales-conversation inputs.
- **[`project-102-positioning-and-gtm-plan`](../../projects/project-102-positioning-and-gtm-plan)** takes the entire artifact set as its ship deliverable — the positioning document, the narrative deck, and the revised headline are the project's core, extended with a demand-gen plan, a motion-design doc, and a 30-60-90 execution plan.

If the artifact set is thin, all five downstream modules inherit the thinness. That is why the evidence bar in this chapter is high.

### The pressure-test — three alternatives, defended

The module's ship discipline includes one specific pressure test: **the positioning must be defended, in writing, against three named competitive alternatives.**

The test is not "can you tell us why you're better than Salesforce." The test is:

- **Name three specific alternatives** from the Component 1 list. Ideally include one direct competitor and two indirect ones (e.g., a spreadsheet, a horizontal tool used in a specific way).
- **For each**, write a paragraph that names: (a) the buyer that would rationally choose the alternative over you, (b) the specific value proposition the alternative offers that you don't, (c) the specific value proposition you offer that the alternative doesn't, (d) the segment where the trade-off tips in your favour (the beachhead).
- **Do not claim to win everywhere.** A positioning that wins against every alternative for every buyer is a positioning that has not made a choice. Real positioning tells you *which buyer, in which segment, considering which alternatives, will rationally pick you* — and *which buyer will rationally pick something else.*

The pressure test is what turns the positioning document from a founder-narrated brief into a decision that can survive contact with a skeptical reader. Exercise 01 (the Dunford five-component drill) and the lab both require this pressure test.

### The dated / signed / versioned discipline (reprised from mod-101 / mod-102)

The same three habits appear in every ship-artifact chapter of this track. Reused here because the discipline is identical:

- **Dated.** Positioning has a shelf life. A positioning document from a year ago may not reflect the current market, current alternatives, current product surface. The header names when.
- **Signed.** Positioning is opinionated; the reader deserves to know whose opinion. The author's name is on the header.
- **Versioned.** Positioning changes rarely — but when it does, the delta between versions is the strategic record. A team without version history has no way to tell whether the current positioning is v1 (never touched) or v4 (three prior positionings tested and abandoned).

### Loomly's strategic-narrative deck (structure)

The deck's slide-by-slide structure (10 core slides + audience-specific close):

1. **Title slide** — "Loomly. Code review automation for mid-market engineering managers." + logo + date.
2. **The shift** — "The rate limit on engineering throughput is no longer code." Chart of PR-cycle-time growth vs. team-size growth from DORA reports.
3. **The enemy** — "The 10-engineer coordination habit doesn't scale to 50." Then vs. now contrast.
4. **The promised land** — "In the world we're building for, engineering managers don't chase PRs." Concrete signals of the promised state.
5. **The evidence** — DORA statistics + Google/GitHub public-case references + Loomly's own beta-cohort cycle-time reduction.
6. **Obstacle 1** — "CODEOWNERS alone isn't enough — you need context."
7. **Obstacle 2** — "Reminders that ping everyone are noise."
8. **Obstacle 3** — "Senior reviewers burn out from over-assignment."
9. **Obstacle 4** — "Escalation is manual — the manager still ends up chasing."
10. **The tools** — Loomly's role, framed as the answer to obstacles 1-4. One slide showing which obstacle each layer of Loomly addresses.
11-13. **Audience-specific close**: for customers, next-steps (demo signup); for AE candidates, deal shape + comp; for investors, raise ask + use of funds.

**Variant A (value + best-for lead):**

> *Loomly picks the right reviewer for every stalled PR and nudges them — so your engineering manager doesn't have to. Built for distributed teams of 20-100 engineers on GitHub.*

> *Loomly watches CODEOWNERS, review history, and current-week load to nudge the right person on every stalled PR. Median review time drops from days to hours.*

### The pressure test — three named alternatives, defended

- **Alternative: manual Slack pings from the engineering manager.** Buyers who choose this: teams under 20 engineers where one manager can hold the whole review-load context in their head. Their value proposition: zero tool cost, zero setup, human judgment. Our value proposition: works at 50-100 engineers where the manager cannot hold context; automated escalation; load-balancing. The trade-off tips in our favour once the team crosses ~25 engineers *and* the manager has explicitly named PR cycle time as a KPI — our beachhead.
- **Alternative: PullReminders / generic Slack reminder apps.** Buyers who choose this: teams that want *any* automation but don't want to think about reviewer selection. Their value: cheaper, simpler, works with any repo. Our value: reviewer selection is intelligent, not random; load-balancing prevents senior burnout; escalation ladder catches stuck PRs. The trade-off tips in our favour when the team has enough PR volume that random pings become noise — typically 15+ open PRs at any time, which correlates with 30+ engineer teams.
- **Alternative: Jellyfish / LinearB (analytics platforms).** Buyers who choose this: engineering VPs at 200+ engineer orgs whose primary need is *reporting to the CFO on engineering throughput*. Their value: comprehensive analytics, DORA-metric dashboards, executive-facing reports. Our value: we don't attempt to compete on analytics; we fix the specific operational bottleneck of reviewer selection and stall recovery. The trade-off tips in our favour when the buyer is the engineering *manager* (not VP), the pain is *operational* (not reporting), and the ACV expectation is $200-500/team/mo (not $15-30/eng/mo). For enterprise-VP buyers, we don't win — and shouldn't try.

The pressure test names *where we win* and, critically, *where we don't*. The Jellyfish/LinearB paragraph is what a positioning without discipline would have said "we're better than" — the discipline is to name the specific segment where we lose and accept it.

## Common failure patterns

- **Combining the three artifacts into one document.** The three have different readers and different decisions. Keeping them separate is what preserves the decision-value of each.
- **Positioning document as marketing copy.** The one-pager is *internal* language — jargon-heavy, technical, opinionated. Marketing copy is Chapter 6's derivation from it.
- **No not-now list.** The beachhead choice isn't binding without the explicit segments being said no to. Founders leave this section blank because it feels risky; blank is the failure.
- **Narrative deck that opens with the product.** The whole Chapter 4 point; if the first slide is the product, the deck has collapsed back into a product-feature deck.
- **One hero variant.** Chapter 6's "always three" rule applies. One variant defends the founder's first draft; three explores the space.
- **Unsourced deck claims.** Every stat, chart, and case reference has a citation. Uncited numbers erode the whole deck's credibility.
- **Not versioning.** Every artifact is dated, signed, versioned. A positioning document without a version number is a document without a shelf-life reader.
- **The pressure test names "we win everywhere."** Real positioning names where you lose. A test result that has you winning every alternative in every segment is a test you failed — you have not made the choice.
- **Delegating to a marketing agency.** Positioning and narrative are strategic decisions; only the founder can commit to them. Agencies produce polished but non-committal artifacts.
- **Shipping the artifact set once and forgetting.** The positioning is revisited quarterly (usually as a light check); the narrative deck evolves with the shift; the hero is A/B tested weekly. The artifact set is *live*, not one-off.

## Summary

- The module's ship deliverable is **three artifacts**, each with a specific reader and decision: **one-page positioning document** (internal brief), **10-15-slide strategic-narrative deck** (persuasive arc), **3-variant revised product-page hero** (buyer-facing).
- The **positioning document** has six non-negotiable sections: alternatives → attributes → value → best-for + beachhead + not-now → category → summary. **One page. Sourced. Opinionated. Dated / signed / versioned.**
- The **narrative deck** carries the Chapter 4 six-part backbone across 10-15 slides. **No product before slide ~8**; audience-appropriate closing slides; one idea per slide; every claim sourced.
- The **hero variants** are always three, each leading on a different Chapter 2 component. **A/B tested** against the operating funnel metric; losing variants do not automatically indict the positioning.
- The **pressure test** is the sharpest discipline: name three alternatives; write who rationally chooses each; name the segment where the trade-off tips in your favour; **name the segments where you lose**. Real positioning tells you where you lose, not just where you win.
- Combining the three artifacts into one collapses the readers and dilutes the decisions. **Keep them separate.**
- The artifact set is **live**: positioning reviewed quarterly, narrative evolved with the shift, hero tested weekly. Not one-off.
- The artifact set is the **input to five downstream modules and two projects**. Thin artifacts → thin everything downstream. The evidence bar in this chapter reflects that.
