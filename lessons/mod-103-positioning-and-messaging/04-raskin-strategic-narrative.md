# The Raskin Strategic Narrative — Shift, Enemy, Promised Land, Evidence, Obstacles, Tools

## Motivation

Chapter 2's five-component positioning is a defensible operating document — it tells the founder, the sales team, and the product team what the product is and who it is for. It does not, by itself, tell a customer *why the world is changing in a way that makes this the moment to buy*.

The strategic-narrative deck exists to do exactly that. Andy Raskin — the strategy consultant who codified the shape in his 2016 Medium essay "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" ([Andy Raskin — "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (2016)](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0)) — argued from a Zuora sales-deck teardown that the highest-performing decks share a specific six-part structure that puts the world's change at the top and the product at the bottom, not the other way around. A product-feature-first deck sells the product; a strategic-narrative deck sells *the world in which the product is inevitable* and, by consequence, the product.

This chapter walks the six parts, names why each is load-bearing, and names why a strategic-narrative deck is more resilient than a product-feature deck across three audiences — first customers, first sales hires, first investors — that a startup has to convince in parallel.

## Core concepts

### Why not a product-feature deck?

The default startup deck opens with the founder team, then the problem, then the product, then the features, then a "traction" slide, then a raise ask. This structure is the norm. It also has three specific weaknesses that the strategic-narrative shape fixes:

- **Feature-first pitches are eminently comparable.** As soon as the deck lists features, the buyer's brain starts a feature-by-feature comparison with the incumbent. Feature comparisons favour the incumbent (they have more features, more polish, more integrations, more brand). The startup loses on any axis except a novelty axis the buyer has not been trained to value yet.
- **Product-first pitches assume the buyer already believes the market is changing.** Most buyers do not. Most buyers are in the status quo — running the spreadsheet, using the incumbent, doing nothing. A product pitched at a buyer whose default is "no decision" fails, because the pitch never establishes urgency.
- **Product-first pitches are fragile across audiences.** The pitch for a first paying customer, a first AE hire, and a first investor is *not the same pitch* if the pitch is product features — the AE cares about deal shape, the customer about pain, the investor about market. The strategic-narrative shape is the same shape for all three; only the *emphasis* varies.

The Raskin narrative fixes all three by placing the *change in the world* at the top of the deck, before the product ever appears. Every subsequent slide asks: *given this world-change is happening, what should you do?* The product appears at the end, as the answer to that question.

### The six-part shape

Raskin's Zuora teardown identified five parts; his follow-up writing (and the practice as it has evolved in the founder community) usually names six by breaking out the *evidence* step from the *promised land* step ([Andy Raskin — "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (2016)](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0); [Andy Raskin — subsequent essays and workshop notes](https://www.andyraskin.com/writing)). The six the module operates on:

| # | Part | The question the slide answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Shift** | What has changed in the world that makes this the moment? |
| 2 | **The Enemy** | Who are the losers of the shift — the incumbents whose model no longer fits? |
| 3 | **The Promised Land** | What does the world look like for the winners of the shift? |
| 4 | **The Evidence** | Why should the audience believe the shift is real and the promised land reachable? |
| 5 | **The Obstacles** | What stands between the audience and the promised land? |
| 6 | **The Tools** | What tools — including yours — allow the audience to overcome the obstacles and reach the promised land? |

This shape is not a template; it is a *sequence of arguments*, each of which the audience must accept before the next one becomes reasonable. Skip any step and the sequence breaks.

### Part 1 — the shift

**The claim:** something in the world has changed that makes the old way of doing things structurally obsolete for a specific group. The change is not incremental; it is a shift in the underlying conditions.

**What qualifies as a shift:**
- A regulatory change that reshapes what buyers are allowed to do (GDPR reshaping consent flows; SOX reshaping financial controls; the EU AI Act reshaping model-governance requirements).
- A technology capability change that makes a previously-impossible workflow possible (large-model inference collapsing the cost of natural-language interfaces; cloud storage collapsing the cost of retention; smartphones collapsing the cost of always-on data).
- A behavioural change in the buyer's customers or workforce that forces the buyer's operating model to adapt (subscription commerce replacing perpetual-license commerce; remote work replacing office-first work; buyer-led sales replacing seller-led sales).
- A structural cost change that makes a previously-uneconomic activity economic (open-source models collapsing model-training costs; managed cloud collapsing infrastructure-hiring costs).

**What does not qualify:**
- **"AI is happening" as a standalone.** Every deck says this in 2026; the buyer nods and forgets. A defensible shift is specific: "The cost of running a domain-specific fine-tuned model has fallen 100× in 18 months," and then trace consequences.
- **A minor product improvement.** "The next generation of X is here" — is not a shift.
- **A founder's opinion about the future.** "We believe the future is Y" — is a wish, not a shift. Shifts are documented; you cite them.
- **Something that has been true for a decade.** "Data is growing exponentially" — is not new. A shift is a recent change with visible downstream consequences.

**How to write it:** name the shift in one sentence. Cite the primary evidence (a research paper, a regulatory filing, a market-size delta, a cost curve). The reader should finish the slide agreeing that the shift is real, not that the founder is clever.

### Part 2 — the enemy

**The claim:** there are specific losers of the shift — incumbents, categories of product, or ways of working that no longer fit the new world. Name them.

The enemy is *not* your direct competitor by name — that mistake collapses the shape into a competitive-pitch deck. The enemy is the *outmoded way of working* that the shift is deprecating. Zuora's original deck named the enemy as "the product economy" — the old world where companies sold perpetual products — versus "the subscription economy" they were pitching. The enemy is a *worldview*, not a company.

**Why naming an enemy matters:**
- It defines the *stakes*. Without an enemy, the shift has no loser and no urgency; the audience can respond "interesting, but why should I move now?"
- It gives the audience a **tribe**. Buyers who identify with "the future" versus "the past" become emotionally invested in the pitch's success; they root for the founder because their identity is aligned.
- It **compresses the sales cycle**. A buyer who accepts the enemy framing has already accepted that the status quo is a losing position; the sale becomes "how do I move," not "why should I move."

**How to write it:** name the enemy in a way that a reasonable audience will accept as legitimate (do not straw-man the incumbent — the buyer knows the incumbent; if you describe it dishonestly you lose credibility). Show one or two ways the enemy's model breaks under the shift. Do not name specific competitor companies; name the worldview or category.

### Part 3 — the promised land

**The claim:** for those on the right side of the shift, here is what the world looks like. Paint the concrete state — what the buyer's business, day, or customer looks like when they have made the transition.

The promised land is *not* your product. It is the *desired end state* the product exists to enable. If the product goes away tomorrow, the promised land is still what the buyer wants. The product is the vehicle; the promised land is the destination.

**Why the promised land is difficult to write well:**
- It must be **specific enough to be believable** ("finance closes the books in three days, not three weeks; the CFO trusts the numbers; the audit committee stops asking for reconciliation memos") — not vague ("your business runs better").
- It must be **desirable enough to motivate change** (the buyer's day is materially better; not marginally).
- It must be **agnostic to the specific tools** used to reach it. The promised land is the outcome; the tools (including yours) are Part 6.
- It must be **legitimate**. If the promised land is unreachable — a fantasy — the audience recognises the pitch as science fiction and disengages.

**How to write it:** name three or four concrete signals of the promised land. Use the buyer's language, not the founder's. Ideally, name an outcome the buyer *has personally wanted for years* — the promised land should feel like the audience's own long-standing wish, articulated back to them.

### Part 4 — the evidence

**The claim:** the shift is real, the promised land is reachable, and there are specific early winners already crossing over. This is the part of the deck that installs *belief*.

**What counts as evidence:**
- **A published or public-domain statistic** that quantifies the shift (an analyst forecast, a research paper, a governmental filing, a widely-cited industry benchmark). Cite the source and the year.
- **A public case of an early winner** — a named company that has already crossed over and captured value from the shift. Ideally a company the audience will find credible (a household name, a known brand in the audience's industry, or an audience-adjacent competitor that has taken the leap).
- **Trend data** — a chart of the underlying shift's velocity (adoption rate, cost curve, regulatory-compliance rate). Charts that come from third parties are more credible than founder-drawn ones.
- **Voice-of-customer quotes** from early adopters — direct language from customers who have already made the transition. This is especially load-bearing if the case-study format is used.

**What does not count:**
- **"We believe X will happen."** Belief is not evidence.
- **The founder team's résumés.** Team quality is Part 6 (or a separate slide), not evidence.
- **Vague "digital transformation is accelerating" stat.** The evidence must be *specific to the shift you named in Part 1*.
- **Fabricated or approximated numbers.** If you cannot cite the source, do not use the number. The moment the audience notices one uncited stat, the whole deck loses credibility.

### Part 5 — the obstacles

**The claim:** between the audience and the promised land, there are specific obstacles — the things that make the transition hard, expensive, or risky. Name them honestly.

**Why naming obstacles is counterintuitive but load-bearing:**
- Buyers **know the obstacles exist**. Pretending they don't makes the pitch feel dishonest. Naming them establishes the founder as a credible ally rather than a salesperson.
- The obstacles set up **the tools** in Part 6. Each tool is the answer to a specific obstacle. Without named obstacles, the tools are unmotivated features.
- Naming obstacles **filters the audience**. Prospects who don't have the obstacles you name are not the ICP; they self-select out. That is a feature.

**How to write it:** name three to five obstacles. Each should be a real, non-trivial barrier the audience has personally felt (technical, organisational, budgetary, or competency-based). Each should be *specific enough that the audience nods*, not generic ("change is hard").

### Part 6 — the tools

**The claim:** here are the tools that, together, allow the audience to overcome the obstacles and reach the promised land. Your product is one of the tools. Ideally the load-bearing one.

**How the tools slide differs from a product-feature slide:**
- **Every tool is framed as the answer to a Part 5 obstacle.** Not a feature list; a numbered response to the earlier problem list.
- **The product is not the *only* tool.** The strategic narrative acknowledges that the audience will use other things too — a data warehouse, an existing CRM, a partner ecosystem, human expertise. This makes the pitch feel realistic and situates the product in the buyer's world.
- **The product's role is named at the level of the buyer's outcome**, not the product's features. "Our product handles X so that Y" — not "our product has features A, B, C, D."

The tools slide is often the shortest slide in the deck. If Parts 1-5 have done their work, the tools are almost self-evident by the time the audience gets to them.

### Why this shape is resilient across three audiences

The strategic-narrative shape works, essentially unchanged, for three distinct audiences a startup has to persuade in parallel — first customers, first sales hires, first investors. Only the *emphasis* varies.

| Audience | What they read the narrative for |
|---|---|
| First customer / buyer | *"Is this the right moment for me to move? Are we on the right side of the shift?"* Weight on Parts 1 (shift), 3 (promised land), 5 (obstacles they already recognise). |
| First AE / sales hire | *"Can I sell this? Does the pitch fit into a discovery-call arc?"* Weight on Parts 2 (enemy — is the sale framed as us-vs-status quo), 5 (obstacle discovery is their sales-conversation raw material), 6 (tool sequence maps to their close plan). |
| First investor | *"Is the shift real and defensible? Is this team the right one to capture the winner-take-most position?"* Weight on Parts 1 (shift — is the market real), 4 (evidence — is the founder's read of the market defensible), and the *founder's* embedded position in the enemy/promised-land framing. |

A product-feature deck cannot serve all three; the AE wants a different deck than the customer wants than the investor wants. The strategic-narrative deck lets one shape serve all three — the sales team drops slides for the customer version; the fundraising team adds the raise slides on the end; the customer version drops the raise; but the six-part backbone is unchanged.

This is the *organising* argument for the shape. A team that ships three separate decks for three separate audiences maintains three separate stories; the moment the stories drift out of sync — the customer hears one story, the investor hears another — the company loses coherence. One backbone; three audience-appropriate emphases.

### Where the narrative sits relative to positioning

The strategic narrative is **downstream of the positioning** (Chapter 2) and **upstream of the messaging** (Chapter 6).

- **Positioning** answers: what is the product, for whom, in what category, against which alternatives, delivering what value.
- **Narrative** answers: why now, what is the world in which this product is inevitable, what change is the buyer participating in.
- **Messaging** answers: what specific words go on what specific surface for what specific audience.

The narrative uses the positioning as its raw material — the enemy is chosen against the alternatives named in Component 1; the promised land articulates the value from Component 3 at world-level scale; the best-for characteristics from Component 4 shape who the deck is addressed to. A narrative built without a positioning is a founder monologue; a positioning without a narrative is a defensible artifact that never persuades anyone.

### Length, cadence, and evolution

- **10-15 slides is typical.** Zuora's referenced deck was 15 slides in one version; the shape does not require slide count as long as the six parts are all present and legible.
- **The narrative is a working document.** Like the positioning, it is revisited when the shift evolves, the enemy fragments, or the promised land reaches broad adoption (at which point the narrative needs to move to the *next* shift).
- **Every founder update, keynote, and All-Hands should echo the narrative.** Consistency across surfaces is what installs the story in the audience's head. A narrative that only lives in the sales deck is a slide; a narrative that lives in the fundraising deck, the recruiting page, the customer-conference keynote, and the CEO's Twitter is a story.

## Concrete example — Loomly's strategic narrative

Return to Loomly. The strategic narrative — written under Positioning A (code-review nudge tool for engineering managers) — might look like:

**Part 1 — The Shift.** *In the last five years, engineering-team size has decoupled from engineering-throughput. Teams have grown 3-5× in headcount while shipping the same or less. The rate-limiting step is no longer code — it's code review. Data from the [DORA state of DevOps reports](https://dora.dev/) shows PR-cycle time has become the leading indicator of engineering throughput.* <!-- needs-research: cite the specific DORA report page and year that documents PR-cycle time as a leading indicator before publishing verbatim -->

**Part 2 — The Enemy.** *The enemy is the assumption that engineering managers can personally coordinate every stalled PR. In a 10-engineer team this scaled. In a 50-engineer distributed team it doesn't — the engineering manager becomes the pager; the reviewer selection is arbitrary; the senior engineers are asked to review everything because their names are the ones the manager knows.*

**Part 3 — The Promised Land.** *In the world we're building for: the engineering manager doesn't chase PRs. Reviews are assigned to the right person the first time. Senior engineers are not overloaded. New reviewers get real experience. Cycle time drops. PRs don't rot for two weeks. The engineering manager gets their afternoon back.*

**Part 4 — The Evidence.** *The DORA metrics show high-performing teams have PR-cycle times under 24 hours; median teams are at 3-5 days. Companies that have automated reviewer selection (see [GitHub's own CODEOWNERS work](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners) and Google's internal Rosie tooling, publicly discussed in [Software Engineering at Google](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book)) have moved cycle time by an order of magnitude. Our beta cohort of six teams has cut median cycle time from 3.2d to 1.1d.* <!-- needs-research: confirm the beta-cohort statistic is real and traceable before shipping the deck externally -->

**Part 5 — The Obstacles.** *(a) CODEOWNERS alone isn't enough — you need context on who last reviewed similar code, and current-week load. (b) Reminders that ping everyone are noise; reminders that ping no one are useless. (c) Senior reviewers burn out from over-assignment; there's no visibility into review-load distribution. (d) Escalation is manual — the engineering manager still ends up chasing.*

**Part 6 — The Tools.** *Loomly is the reviewer-selection and nudge layer that plugs into GitHub, watches CODEOWNERS + review history + current-week load, picks the right reviewer, throttles nudges to prevent overload, and escalates unattended PRs after 72 hours. It is one tool among several — you still need CODEOWNERS defined; you still need a code-review culture; you still need a manager who cares about cycle time — but Loomly is the load-bearing piece that turns those inputs into reduced cycle time.*

The narrative fits on 10-12 slides. The product is on the last slide. The audience — a mid-market engineering manager — spent 8 slides agreeing with the world-view before the product appeared, at which point the product is the *obvious* answer to obstacles they themselves have named.

Now consider the same narrative delivered to three audiences:

- **To a mid-market engineering-manager prospect:** every slide as above. The prospect leaves the meeting having agreed to a beta trial.
- **To a first AE candidate:** same six slides, plus one added slide showing the sales-cycle arc from cold outreach → discovery call (which surfaces the Part 5 obstacles from the prospect's own mouth) → demo → close. The AE reads the deck as a sales script and can imagine running it.
- **To a seed investor:** same six slides, plus a slide inserting DORA-adjacent market sizing (how many engineering managers of teams sized 20-100 exist globally), a slide on the founder team's engineering-productivity background, and a slide with the raise ask. The investor evaluates whether the shift is real (Part 1) and whether the team is the right one to capture the winner-take-most position given the shift.

One backbone. Three audience-appropriate emphases. Coherent across surfaces.

## Common failure patterns

- **Opening with the team or the product.** The audience has not yet accepted that the market is changing; the product has no context. Move the shift to slide 1.
- **Naming a specific competitor company as the enemy.** The enemy is a *worldview* the audience should reject, not a company you compete with. Naming a competitor collapses the deck into a competitive-pitch shape.
- **Promised land that is your product.** The promised land is the *desired end state*; the product is Part 6, the vehicle. If the product goes away tomorrow, the promised land is still the outcome the buyer wants.
- **Evidence that is not evidence.** "We believe X" is not evidence. Cite a source. Vague or fabricated statistics erode the whole deck's credibility.
- **Obstacles that are generic.** "Change is hard" is not an obstacle. Name three-to-five specific, felt barriers the audience will nod at.
- **The tools slide is a feature list.** If Parts 1-5 have done their work, the tools are almost self-evident. Frame each tool as the answer to a Part 5 obstacle, not as a feature.
- **Different decks for different audiences.** The narrative shape should be the same for customer, AE, and investor; only the emphasis and closing slides vary. Maintaining three separate stories fractures the company's coherence.
- **The narrative is a one-off artifact.** If the strategic narrative only lives in the sales deck, it is not a narrative — it is a slide. It has to echo in All-Hands, in the recruiting page, in the CEO's public writing.
- **Skipping the positioning work and going straight to the deck.** The narrative uses the Chapter 2 positioning as raw material. A narrative built without positioning is a founder monologue that will not persuade anyone.

## Summary

- The **strategic-narrative deck** is the founder-authored six-part story that places the world's change at the top and the product at the bottom, not the other way around.
- **Six parts in order**: the shift (what changed), the enemy (who loses), the promised land (what the winner's world looks like), the evidence (why to believe), the obstacles (what stands between the audience and the promised land), the tools (including yours, that clear the obstacles).
- The shape is **resilient across audiences** — first customer, first AE hire, first investor — because each audience weights the same six-part backbone differently. One backbone; three emphases.
- The **enemy is a worldview**, not a competitor. Naming a specific competitor collapses the deck into a competitive-pitch shape.
- The **promised land is the buyer's desired end state**, not the product. The product is a Part 6 vehicle, not the destination.
- **Evidence is cited**. If a statistic cannot be sourced, it does not go in the deck. One uncited number erodes the whole narrative's credibility.
- The narrative is **downstream of the positioning** (Chapter 2) and **upstream of the messaging** (Chapter 6). It uses the positioning's alternatives, value, and best-for as raw material.
- The narrative is a **working document**, echoed across every founder-facing surface — sales deck, All-Hands, recruiting page, keynote — not a one-off artifact.
