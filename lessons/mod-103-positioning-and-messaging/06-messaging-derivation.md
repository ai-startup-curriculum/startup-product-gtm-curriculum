# Messaging Derivation — From Locked Positioning to Words on the Page

## Motivation

Chapter 1 named the distinction; Chapters 2-5 built the positioning, the failure-mode diagnostic, the strategic narrative, and the beachhead. This chapter closes the loop by walking the derivation from a *locked* positioning to the specific words on the specific surfaces the buyer sees.

Messaging is where positioning meets the world. If Chapters 2-5 do their work, the messaging is *derivable* — a PMM or founder can write hero copy, subheads, feature messaging, and sales-script beats in a session, because the positioning constrains the space. If Chapters 2-5 have not done their work, the messaging is *invented* — the PMM writes something plausible-sounding that the sales team quietly rewrites and the ads-vendor rewrites again, and the company ends up with four inconsistent voices across surfaces.

This chapter is short by design. It is not a copywriting tutorial; it is the *derivation protocol* that turns positioning into buyer-facing words, and the *test-cadence* discipline that lets you iterate messaging weekly without disturbing the positioning under it.

## Core concepts

### The derivation protocol

Every messaging surface — hero headline, subhead, feature messaging, sales-email subject line, ad creative, pitch-deck slide title — is derived from the same five-component positioning through the same steps:

1. **Pick the surface + audience + moment.** A hero for the product page seen by a first-time visitor is not the same messaging surface as the "closing slide" of a sales deck seen by a buyer at end-of-cycle. Different constraint set; different derivation.
2. **Pick the load-bearing component for this surface.** Hero copy usually leads on **value** (from Component 3). A category-page listing leads on **market category** (Component 5). A competitive comparison page leads on **unique attributes** (Component 2). A homepage subhead usually names **best-for characteristics** (Component 4) as a filter. Different surfaces put different components at the top.
3. **Compress to the surface's constraint (length, tone, channel).** Hero copy is one sentence, ideally under 12 words. A sales-email subject line is one line, ideally under 8 words. A pitch-deck slide title is 4-8 words. The surface constraint is real; do not violate it.
4. **Write three variants.** Never one. The purpose of three is that it forces you to explore the space rather than defending your first draft. Chapter 7 (and Exercise 06) ship three variants for exactly this reason.
5. **Pressure-test against the buyer's language.** The messaging is not the founder's language; it is the *buyer's* language, filtered through the positioning. Every claim on a surface should either be verbatim buyer language (from mod-101 discovery interviews or mod-102 very-disappointed free-text) or a compression of it. Founder-invented language is the death of messaging.
6. **Test the surface.** Once shipped, iterate weekly on the specific surface — A/B test hero headlines, subject lines, CTA text. This is the *messaging* cadence, not the *positioning* cadence.

The critical discipline: **when a variant tests poorly, first ask whether the positioning is wrong.** If three consecutive hero variants underperform, the answer is not "write a fourth hero variant." The answer is to run the Chapter 3 failure-mode diagnostic. Persistent messaging under-performance is a positioning symptom.

### The three canonical messaging surfaces this chapter operates on

The exercises in this module (particularly Exercise 06) focus on three messaging surfaces that are the highest-leverage in early-stage GTM. Every other surface derives similarly.

1. **The product-page hero** — the first thing an inbound visitor reads. Headline + subhead + primary CTA. Ideally under 20 seconds of reading time; ideally comprehensible in a 3-second glance.
2. **The pitch-deck opener** — the first slide (or the first 2-3 slides) of a sales deck. Sets the strategic-narrative shift (Chapter 4). Same audience-emphasis logic as the narrative.
3. **The sales-email cold-outbound opening line** — the first sentence of an outbound sales email. Not a headline; a hook that establishes relevance in the recipient's inbox in 5 seconds. Same source (positioning) but a completely different surface constraint.

### The product-page hero — headline + subhead + CTA

The most-derived messaging surface. Its structure is nearly fixed; only the words vary.

**Headline (1 line, 5-12 words):** compresses the *value* (Component 3) or names the *best-for + value* (Component 4 + 3). Never a category label alone (that would leave the value unmentioned); never a comparative superlative without an attribute (that is Chapter 3 Mode 2).

Templates that tend to work — not templates to follow blindly, but shapes to derive from:

- **`[Value] for [best-for]`** — "Automated code review nudges for engineering managers." Names value + best-for; category is implicit.
- **`[Enemy-of-status-quo] for [best-for]`** — "Stop being the reviewer nag." Frames the buyer's current pain; assumes the audience recognises the pain from Component 3.
- **`[Verb-outcome] with [category / attribute]`** — "Ship faster with the right reviewer nudged at the right time." Value + attribute; category is implicit.

Templates to avoid:

- **`[Category name]. [Category name].`** — "Developer productivity. Reimagined." Names the category with no value; hits Chapter 3 Mode 3.
- **`The [comparative] [category]`** — "The smartest project manager." Comparative superlative without an attribute; Chapter 3 Mode 2.
- **`AI-powered [category]`** — "AI-powered developer productivity." "AI-powered" is 2026 background; adds no differentiation.
- **`[Company name] is the [long noun phrase]`** — "Loomly is the developer collaboration fabric that…" Long, category-invented, category-confused.

**Subhead (1-2 lines, 15-30 words):** derives what the headline did not. If the headline led on value, the subhead adds best-for characteristics or the unique attribute that produces the value. The subhead's job is to answer the reader's silent next question: *"okay, but what does this actually do, and is it for me?"*

**Primary CTA (2-4 words):** the specific action the visitor should take. The CTA is derived from the sales motion (mod-107 will pick this up more thoroughly). PLG products have "Sign up free"; SDR-AE products have "Talk to sales"; enterprise products have "Get a demo." The CTA is a *sales-motion signal*, not a copywriting choice.

**Secondary content (below the fold):** the "how" — the specific unique attributes and their traced value chains. This is where Component 2 (attributes) and Component 3 (value) get their fuller expression. The below-the-fold content is longer and can be structured as a features + benefits list, but each item still has to trace attribute → value.

### The pitch-deck opener

Chapter 4's strategic-narrative sequence *is* the pitch-deck derivation. The first slide is the shift (Part 1); the next few are enemy → promised land → evidence. The messaging on those slides is the compressed narrative.

**Slide 1 (the shift) messaging constraints:**
- Title: names the shift in 5-10 words. Not the product name; the world's change.
- Body: one image (a chart of the shift, or a photograph of the promised land) or one sentence naming the specific change with a citation.
- No product mention. The product does not appear until slide ~8-10.

**Slide 2 (the enemy) messaging constraints:**
- Title: names the outmoded worldview.
- Body: shows the enemy's model breaking under the shift. Often a "then vs. now" contrast, or a chart showing the enemy's model's diminishing returns.

The point is that the deck messaging is *derived from the narrative structure*, which is derived from the positioning. If the positioning changes, the narrative changes, and the deck opener changes. Building a deck from scratch every time — writing whatever the founder feels like this quarter — is the anti-pattern.

### The cold-outbound opening line

A completely different surface constraint from either of the above. The reader is scanning a full inbox; you have 3-5 seconds to earn the next 15 seconds.

**What the opening line has to do:**
- Establish *relevance* — the recipient must recognise, in the first phrase, that this is about their situation, not a generic template. This is where the beachhead segment (Chapter 5) does its work — the opening line names the segment specifically.
- Reference a *specific signal* — a recent hiring push, a recent funding announcement, a recent product release, a specific piece of writing the recipient published. Generic openers ("Hi, I saw your company and thought…") are deleted.
- Compress the *value* into a phrase — not the full pitch; a hook that makes the recipient want to read the next line.

**Template shapes:**
- **`[Specific signal] usually means [pain]. We help [best-for] with [value].`** — "The Series-B announcement usually means your engineering team is about to double. Loomly helps mid-market eng managers keep PR-cycle time under 24 hours as the team scales."
- **`Saw [specific artifact]. Curious how you're handling [pain the artifact implies].`** — "Saw your engineering-blog post on refactoring the CI pipeline. Curious how you're handling PR-review load as the team's grown."

**What to avoid:**
- **The generic template opener.** "I hope this finds you well." Deleted.
- **The founder's résumé.** "I'm the CEO of…" Nobody cares.
- **The multi-paragraph pitch.** Nobody reads that far in a cold email.

The mod-105 (founder-led sales) content will pick up cold-outbound in operational depth; this module's contribution is that the opening-line messaging derives from *the same positioning* as the hero copy and the deck opener. One source; three surfaces; three different derivations.

### Consistency across surfaces — the "same story, different surface" test

The single test that reveals whether messaging is *derived* or *invented*: **pick a claim from your positioning, and check that the same claim appears — in surface-appropriate compression — across every messaging surface.**

Example: Loomly's positioning says the load-bearing value is "engineering managers stop being the pager for stalled reviews."

- Hero headline: *"Stop being the reviewer nag."*
- Subhead: *"Loomly picks the right reviewer for every stalled PR and nudges them so your eng manager doesn't have to."*
- Deck slide 3 (promised land): *"In the world we're building for, engineering managers don't chase PRs."*
- Cold-email opening: *"The Series-B announcement usually means you're about to double eng and the manager becomes the pager. We're the layer that fixes that."*
- Sales-demo closing line: *"You'll get your afternoons back."*
- Twitter tagline: *"Stop chasing PRs."*
- Recruiting-page mission line: *"We're building the tool that lets engineering managers stop being the pager."*

Same claim; six surfaces; six compressions. That is derivation working. When the claim on the hero contradicts the claim in the deck contradicts the claim in the recruiting page, the company has *inventing* messaging — the founder is writing whatever seems clever on each surface. The remedy is not "hire a better copywriter"; the remedy is to lock the positioning and derive from it.

### The messaging test cadence

- **Hero copy: monthly review, weekly A/B tests.** The hero is the most-instrumented surface; treat it as a live experiment.
- **Deck opener: quarterly review.** Deck iteration lags hero iteration because decks are used in-meeting; feedback comes slower.
- **Cold-outbound openers: weekly iteration on templates, per-recipient customisation per send.** The cadence is fast because volume is high.
- **Secondary product-page content, blog headlines, ad creative: as needed.** Instrument for CTR; iterate on losers.
- **Positioning: quarterly review at most; usually annual.** If you find yourself considering changing the positioning to fix a messaging test failure, run Chapter 3's failure-mode diagnostic first; the positioning is often correct and the surface-specific message needs another variant.

The mental model: **fast on messaging; slow on positioning.** The whole point of the module's Chapter 1 distinction is to make this cadence legal — you *should* iterate messaging weekly, and you *should not* iterate positioning weekly. If you're doing the reverse, the module has not landed.

### Anti-patterns in messaging derivation

- **"Let's copy [competitor]'s hero."** Positioning is what makes the hero coherent; copying the words without the underlying positioning produces an incoherent surface.
- **The one-off "brand refresh."** If the brand refresh changes the messaging without touching the positioning, it is a wallpaper change. If it changes the positioning too, it should be treated as a positioning re-authoring — not a marketing project.
- **The "AI-powered" retrofit.** Adding "AI-powered" or "AI-native" to the hero copy without changing the underlying attribute → value chain is not messaging; it is decoration.
- **The customer-quote hero.** Hero copy that is a customer quote ("'This changed our team' — Jane, CTO") is a testimonial, not a value proposition. Use it below the fold; do not use it as the primary hero claim.
- **The mission-statement hero.** "Empowering the world's developers to ship faster" — is a mission. It says nothing about what the product does, for whom, or against what alternative. Every visitor bounces.
- **The founder-monologue subhead.** Subheads that read as "we started this company because…" are for the About page, not the hero.

## Concrete example — Loomly's messaging surfaces, derived

Given Loomly's Chapter 2 positioning and Chapter 4 narrative, three canonical surfaces:

### Product-page hero (three variants — the "always three" rule)

**Variant A (value + best-for lead):**
> *Stop being the reviewer nag.*
> *Loomly picks the right reviewer for every stalled PR and nudges them — so your engineering manager doesn't have to. Built for distributed teams of 20-100 engineers on GitHub.*
> `[Get a demo]`

**Variant B (attribute-first lead):**
> *Right reviewer. Right time. No nagging.*
> *Loomly watches your CODEOWNERS, review history, and current-week load to nudge the right person on every stalled PR. Median review time drops from days to hours.*
> `[Get a demo]`

**Variant C (enemy-of-status-quo lead):**
> *Your PRs stopped rotting.*
> *Loomly replaces the weekly PR-triage meeting with automated reviewer selection and load-balanced nudges. Engineering managers get their afternoons back; senior reviewers stop being overloaded.*
> `[Get a demo]`

All three trace to the same positioning. Each leads with a different component (value; attribute; promised land) but each derives from Chapter 2. Variant A is the safest for cold traffic; B for a technically-oriented audience; C for a promised-land-primed audience arriving from the strategic-narrative deck. A/B test between them; the winner tells you which framing the beachhead responds to.

### Pitch-deck opener (slides 1-2)

**Slide 1 — the shift:**
> Title: *"The rate limit on eng throughput is no longer code."*
> Body: A chart showing PR-cycle-time growth vs. team-size growth across a sample of engineering orgs, sourced from DORA reports. <!-- needs-research: confirm the specific DORA report page cited before shipping externally -->
> No product mention.

**Slide 2 — the enemy:**
> Title: *"The 10-engineer coordination habit doesn't scale to 50."*
> Body: A "then vs. now" contrast — small teams coordinated via standup and Slack pings; large distributed teams need something else. One sentence naming that most 50-engineer orgs still coordinate the same way as 10-engineer orgs did.
> No product mention.

Both slides derive from the Chapter 4 narrative, which derives from the Chapter 2 positioning. Neither has "Loomly" on it. Loomly appears on slide ~8-10, as one of several tools that clear the Part-5 obstacles.

### Cold-outbound opener

**To an engineering manager at a Series-B-announced 60-engineer devtools company:**

> *"Congrats on the Series B — that usually means your eng team is about to double. Two of your senior engineers are already reviewing 40% of the PRs, and it gets worse as the team grows. Loomly's the layer that fixes reviewer selection so your top reviewers stop being the bottleneck. Worth 20 minutes to see if the pain is familiar?"*

The opener names a specific recent signal (Series B), a specific pain the beachhead recognises (senior-reviewer over-load), the value in one clause, and a low-commitment ask. Derived, not invented.

All three surfaces tell the *same story* in surface-appropriate compressions. That is what derivation looks like.

## Common failure patterns

- **Writing the hero before locking the positioning.** The hero rewrites for a quarter and moves nothing. Chapter 3's failure-mode diagnostic is the antidote.
- **Copying competitor messaging.** The competitor's messaging is derived from their positioning; without the positioning underneath, the words are incoherent.
- **Inconsistent claims across surfaces.** The hero says one thing; the deck says another; the recruiting page says a third. The company loses coherence; the buyer notices; trust collapses.
- **One variant only.** Never one. Always three. Forces exploration of the space instead of defending the first draft.
- **Founder-invented language.** The hero uses phrases no customer has ever used. The messaging tests badly because it doesn't resonate. Sourced buyer language (from mod-101 corpus or mod-102 free-text) is the antidote.
- **Iterating positioning to fix messaging tests.** The positioning is usually not the problem when one hero variant tests badly; write another variant. If *three consecutive* variants all fail, *then* diagnose the positioning.
- **Multi-paragraph hero copy.** Nobody reads that. Cut to one sentence + subhead.
- **Category-only hero.** "Developer productivity." Says nothing about the value, the best-for, or the alternative. The visitor bounces.
- **Adding "AI-powered" as differentiation.** In 2026 it is background, not a differentiator. If the AI does something specific and valuable, name the specific thing.

## Summary

- Messaging is a **derivation** from a locked positioning, not a substitute for one.
- Every messaging surface follows the same **six-step derivation**: pick surface + audience + moment; pick load-bearing component; compress to surface constraint; write three variants; pressure-test against buyer language; ship and test.
- The three canonical early-stage messaging surfaces are the **product-page hero**, the **pitch-deck opener**, and the **cold-outbound opening line**. Each has a different surface constraint but the same positioning source.
- **Consistency across surfaces** is the test that messaging is derived, not invented. A positioning claim should appear — in surface-appropriate compression — on every messaging surface without contradicting itself.
- The **change cadence** is fast on messaging (weekly A/B tests), slow on positioning (quarterly or annual review). Chapter 1's distinction makes this legal.
- **Three variants, always** — never one. Forces exploration; prevents defending the first draft.
- Persistent messaging under-performance is a **positioning symptom**, not a messaging problem. Run Chapter 3's diagnostic before writing a fourth variant.
- **Sourced buyer language** is the raw material — from the mod-101 corpus, the mod-102 very-disappointed free-text, or ongoing customer conversations. Founder-invented language is the death of messaging.
