# The Founding-Sales Stage Model — End-to-End

## Motivation

There is a specific class of selling that a founder has to do herself, before the first sales hire, that is *not* the same activity a career AE will do six months later on the same product. Peter Kazanjy — Atrium co-founder, founder of the Modern Sales Pros community, and author of *Founding Sales* — named this activity "founding sales" and specified the stage model this module runs end-to-end: **prospect → outreach → discovery → demo → proposal → close → next-step discipline**, plus the CRM hygiene that keeps the whole loop honest.

Founding sales is a distinct motion because the seller is the person who built the product, who wrote the mod-101 discovery report, who authored the mod-104 ICP, and who is going to hand the whole apparatus to the first AE when the motion is repeatable. The founder can — and should — do things a career AE cannot: change the price on the call, re-scope the product on the call, kill an in-flight feature that a customer's demo response says is worthless, promise to build a specific integration and have it shipped by the following Tuesday. The founding-sales motion is *inseparable* from the product / pricing / positioning motion — every call is simultaneously a sale and a customer-development interview and a pricing test and a positioning stress-test.

The failure mode this module exists to catch: **the founder either (a) refuses to sell — "I'm a builder, not a salesperson" — and hires a VP Sales too early, before the founder has personally closed the first 20-30 customers and understands the win-loss pattern; or (b) sells opportunistically, without a stage model, without CRM discipline, without a discovery script, and produces a pile of closed-won revenue with no repeatable pattern the first AE can inherit.** Both failure modes cost 12-18 months and, empirically, the sales hire that was made without the founder having done the work.

The remedy this module ships: the founder runs the six-stage motion by hand for the first 20-30 customers, produces a *pack* of artifacts — cold outreach sequence, discovery script, demo checklist, proposal template, next-step email templates, CRM stage definitions — that the first AE inherits, and hands the motion over only when the diagnostic signals in Chapter 8 fire.

This first chapter fixes the shape of the whole motion. Chapters 2-8 build each stage individually.

## Core concepts

### The operating definition

**Founding sales** is the founder-driven, customer-development-fused, artifact-producing sales motion that closes the first 20-30 paying customers by hand and produces the reusable stage-by-stage instruments (script, checklist, template, CRM shape) that make the motion transferrable to a first AE hire.

Three words in that definition are load-bearing.

- **Founder-driven** — the founder is on every discovery call and demo. Not the CTO, not a technical co-founder who "doesn't want to sell," not an outside sales consultant, not a VP Sales hired in month 4. If founder-led sales delegates the front of the motion before the motion is characterised, the delegate produces revenue without characterisation and the motion never becomes transferrable.
- **Customer-development-fused** — every discovery call is simultaneously a mod-101-style problem interview (the founder is still learning who has the pain and how they describe it), a mod-102 PMF-signal probe (does the prospect show the pull-signature of a strong-fit customer, or the polite-interest signature of a weak-fit), a mod-103 positioning stress-test (does the buyer put the product on the shelf you claim to be on), a mod-104 ICP score (in / out / partial), and a mod-106 pricing test (does the price land as obviously-cheap, obviously-fair, or obviously-expensive). Career-AE sales is sales; founder sales is sales + development.
- **Artifact-producing** — every stage of the motion produces a specific reusable artifact. The point is not the revenue in isolation; the point is the *repeatable script* the first AE inherits. A founder who closes 30 customers and can hand the AE only their own tacit knowledge has done half the work.

### Kazanjy's stage model — the six stages

Kazanjy's founding-sales stage model is a linear pipeline of six stages. Each stage has a defined entry condition, exit condition, artifact, and next-step discipline.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE                    │  ENTRY                 │  EXIT (or dq)    │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  1. PROSPECT              │  ICP list-build        │  named account   │
│     (list, research)      │                        │  + persona ID    │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  2. OUTREACH              │  named account         │  first meeting   │
│     (cold email, cold     │  + persona            │  booked          │
│      call, warm intro)    │                        │                  │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  3. DISCOVERY             │  first meeting         │  qualified in /  │
│     (find + size pain,    │  scheduled             │  qualified out   │
│      qualify buyer)       │                        │  + next step     │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  4. DEMO                  │  qualified in +        │  buyer sees pain │
│     (confirm pain,        │  agreed demo           │  addressed +     │
│      show pain addressed) │  audience              │  next step       │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  5. PROPOSAL              │  buyer + champion +    │  paper out       │
│     (price, terms,        │  economic buyer        │  + close date    │
│      scope, close plan)   │  aligned               │  + next step     │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  6. CLOSE                 │  paper out             │  signed +        │
│     (paper, procurement,  │                        │  invoiced +      │
│      countersign)         │                        │  kickoff booked  │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The stages are named in the founder's CRM (Chapter 7 develops the CRM discipline) and every open opportunity lives in exactly one stage at a time. A deal that "isn't really in Discovery but also isn't in Demo" is a symptom that (a) the previous stage's exit criterion was not met or (b) the current stage's entry criterion was not confirmed. Both are fixable — with next-step discipline (Chapter 5) — but only if the stage model is enforced.

### Founding sales vs. career-AE sales — the four axis differences

The founding-sales motion is not "AE sales, done by the founder." It is a different activity along four axes.

1. **Objective.** Career-AE objective: hit quota. Founder objective: characterise the sale so it can be handed off. Quota-hitting is a *by-product*; characterisation is the *product*. The founder who optimises for closed-won-this-quarter often does so by taking non-ICP deals (Chapter 6 develops the anti-pattern) that inflate revenue and pollute the characterisation.
2. **Authority.** Career AE has authority to discount within a band, quote from a rate card, and quote a scope from a scope-of-work menu. Founder has *unbounded* authority — she can change the price, re-scope the product, promise a feature, cancel the contract. This is a superpower and a liability. Superpower: she can close a deal a career AE cannot. Liability: she carries no rate card discipline, and the deals she closes may not be sellable by anyone but her.
3. **Time budget.** Career AE spends 100% of her time selling. Founder spends 20-40% of her time selling (the rest is building the product, hiring the team, running the company, raising the round). The motion has to be designed around this reality — a founder who thinks she can run a 200-account outbound sequence with 12 discovery calls a week is deceiving herself about the calendar (Chapter 2 develops this).
4. **Feedback loop.** Career AE's feedback loop closes at the deal review: won or lost, reason coded, move on. Founder's feedback loop closes into the *product*, the *ICP*, the *positioning*, and the *pricing* — every won deal changes the roadmap, every lost deal changes the ICP scorecard, every pricing-shock reaction changes the pricing pack. The feedback loop is what makes founding sales worth doing directly, and what makes delegating too early a strategic mistake.

### The three artifact tiers

The pack of artifacts founding sales produces splits into three tiers.

**Tier 1 — per-stage instruments (the first AE picks these up on day one):**

- **Cold outreach sequence** (Chapter 2, Exercise 01) — 4-6 touch sequence with copy, timing, personalisation slots, and measured reply-rate + positive-reply targets.
- **Discovery call script** (Chapter 3, Exercises 02-03) — SPICED or MEDDIC arc, the exact 8-12 questions, the disqualifier hooks, the transition to demo or to close-lost.
- **Demo checklist** (Chapter 4, Exercise 04) — the pre-demo confirmation questions, the demo agenda, the anti-demo-pattern checklist, the post-demo next-step.
- **Proposal template** (Chapter 6) — the scope, the price, the terms, the champion enablement, the countersign path.
- **Next-step email templates** (Chapter 5, Exercise 05) — the "here's what we agreed / here's the next step" email at each stage transition, with the specific ask embedded.

**Tier 2 — the operating instruments (the founder + AE use together):**

- **CRM stage definitions** (Chapter 7, Exercise 06) — what puts a deal in Discovery vs. Demo vs. Proposal, what puts it back a stage, what closes it lost.
- **Weekly forecast** (Chapter 7) — honest commit / best-case / pipeline math, per-deal, per-stage.
- **Win/loss reason codes** (Chapter 7) — the small, disciplined list of close-lost reasons that roll up to ICP feedback (mod-104), pricing feedback (mod-106), and product feedback (roadmap).

**Tier 3 — the hand-off instruments (the founder ships these to the first AE):**

- **First-hire readiness checklist** (Chapter 8) — the diagnostic signals that say the motion is repeatable and it is time to hire.
- **AE ramp plan** (Chapter 8, forward-linked to mod-110) — the 60-90 day plan for the first AE to inherit the pack.
- **Founder-out plan** (Chapter 8) — the specific rules for what the founder still does after hiring (deals above ACV threshold X, deals in strategic segment Y) and what she stops doing (everything else).

Every module exercise ships at least one of these artifacts. The module's cumulative output is the *pack* itself.

### The four gates — where founder-led sales usually breaks

Founder-led sales does not usually fail because the founder cannot sell. It fails at four specific gates.

- **The prospect / outreach gate.** The founder never runs a disciplined outbound sequence — she waits for inbound, or sends 200 personalised emails once and gives up when the reply rate is 2%. Chapter 2 fixes this.
- **The discovery-to-demo gate.** The founder skips discovery entirely — a prospect asks for a demo on the first call and gets one, without the founder having found or sized the pain. Every demo becomes a feature tour, no next step is defensible, and the motion becomes indistinguishable from a product-marketing website (Chapters 3-4 fix this).
- **The next-step gate.** The founder ends a call with "let me follow up next week" instead of a calendared next step, and the deal enters pipeline debt. Weeks later the deal is technically open, technically not close-lost, and definitely not going to close. Chapter 5 fixes this.
- **The close-and-forecast gate.** The founder cannot honestly say which of her 30 open deals will close this quarter, so the forecast is either wildly optimistic (every open deal is "hot") or wildly pessimistic (only signed deals count). Chapter 7 fixes this.

Each of these gates has a specific artifact and a specific discipline. The point of running the six-stage motion end-to-end is that the founder is forced to build the instrument that gets past each gate — instead of only working the gates she is naturally comfortable with.

### The pack's downstream dependencies

The module produces the pack, but the pack has dependencies. If the upstream modules are missing, the pack is authored on sand.

- **mod-101 discovery report** — the founder needs to know the pain shape before she can author a discovery script. Without mod-101, the discovery call's SPICED "Pain" field is populated from founder intuition and will not match how buyers actually describe the pain.
- **mod-102 PMF signal** — the founder needs to know whether a "yes" from a given segment is a pull-signature yes or a polite-interest yes. Without mod-102, closed-won revenue accumulates without a durability signal and the pack accumulates false learnings from customers who will churn in six months.
- **mod-103 positioning + narrative** — the founder needs to know what shelf the buyer will put the product on, and the strategic narrative that frames the demo. Without mod-103, the demo is a feature tour and the buyer's reaction is "cool, we'll think about it."
- **mod-104 ICP + personas + disqualifier checklist** — the discovery call *is* the ICP scorecard in motion. Without mod-104, the founder qualifies from intuition; the pack cannot be handed to an AE who lacks the intuition.

Founder-led sales without the upstream is possible — many founders run it — but it produces the "we closed 20 customers and cannot say why some worked and some churned" pattern. The module's operating assumption is that mod-101-104 exist (in some form — synthesized or real) before this module runs.

### The three-view rubric — a shippable pack serves three audiences

Like the ICP artifact in mod-104, the founder-led-sales pack has to serve three internal audiences to earn the "shippable" label.

1. **The founder herself, next quarter.** Her memory of what worked at week 4 will be unreliable by month 4. The pack is a memory instrument. Every artifact must be re-usable by the founder six months later without re-derivation.
2. **The first AE hire.** She reads the pack on day one and can run the motion by end of week 6. Every artifact must be legible without the founder in the room.
3. **The next-round investors.** They read the pack (or the compressed version of it — see mod-110's GTM one-pager) and can distinguish "founder has characterised the motion" from "founder has generated some closed-won revenue." A pack that is not investor-legible does not distinguish founder-led sales from founder-led firefighting.

An artifact that serves only the founder is a diary; one that serves only the AE is a training manual; one that serves only the investor is a slide. The shippable pack serves all three.

### The 20-30 customer number — where it comes from

The "first 20-30 paying customers" number is not arbitrary. It is the empirical band where three things converge:

- **Sample size for a repeatable win-loss pattern.** Below ~10 wins the founder cannot distinguish signal from noise on why deals won. Below ~10 losses she cannot see the disqualifier pattern. 20-30 total gives ~15-20 wins and ~10-15 losses, enough for a legible pattern.
- **Sample size for a stable discovery script.** By deal 15-20 the founder is running roughly the same discovery script call to call; new question additions become rare. Below that, the script is still being written on the fly.
- **Sample size for a defensible ACV.** Below ~10 wins the ACV is one-shot noise; by 20-30 the deal-size distribution has a real median and a real high-low band that the pricing pack (mod-106) can be anchored against.

Some categories (very low-ACV self-serve; very high-ACV enterprise land-and-expand) shift this number. A $200/month SaaS product may need 200 customers to build the pattern; a $500K enterprise deal may need 5-10. The 20-30 band is the mid-market working default. Chapter 8's first-hire diagnostic uses the *shape* of the pattern, not the customer count, as the hand-off trigger.

## Concrete example — the stage model on a single Loomly deal

Loomly (the running mid-market devtools example from mod-103 / mod-104) has an ACV around $12k / year and a 4-8 week sales cycle. The founder runs one deal through the full six-stage motion:

- **Stage 1 — Prospect.** Founder pulls a 15-account list from LinkedIn Sales Navigator against the ICP filter (50-150 engineers, B2B SaaS devtools / observability, GitHub primary). For each account she identifies a VP Engineering by name and a champion-candidate Engineering Manager by name.
- **Stage 2 — Outreach.** Founder runs a 4-touch cold sequence over 12 days: personalised email 1, follow-up 2 with a specific angle, LinkedIn touch, email 3 with a break-up ask. Of 15 accounts, 2 book a first meeting. One is Acme Observability, VP Eng Ramesh; the other is Beta Data Platform, EM Priya.
- **Stage 3 — Discovery (Acme).** Founder runs a 30-minute discovery call with Ramesh. She works the SPICED arc (Chapter 3): situation (50-eng B2B SaaS observability, GitHub, distributed), pain (PR review times drifted from 8 hours to 3 days over Q3), impact ($240K estimated lost deploy velocity), critical event (Q4 board deck has a cycle-time goal), decision (Ramesh is buyer, EM Sasha is champion, no procurement gate < $15K). Verdict: qualified in; next step: 45-minute demo with Sasha in the room, booked before end of call for the following Tuesday.
- **Stage 4 — Demo.** Founder runs the 45-minute demo per the demo checklist (Chapter 4): 5 minutes recap of Ramesh's stated pain, 20 minutes of pain-addressing product usage with Sasha driving, 10 minutes of ROI framing against the $240K estimate, 10 minutes of pricing walk-through and objection surfacing. Next step: proposal in 48 hours, Ramesh confirms he will bring in his CFO for economic-buyer sign-off within a week.
- **Stage 5 — Proposal.** Founder sends a 3-page proposal (Chapter 6 template): scope (unlimited users at the 100-eng tier), price ($12K / year, quarterly billed), terms (30-day free trial, opt-out clause 30 days into contract), close plan (Ramesh redlines this week, CFO signs off next Tuesday, procurement lightweight, kickoff week following). Every commitment has a date.
- **Stage 6 — Close.** Two weeks after proposal, Ramesh signs; CFO countersigns two days later; invoice out; kickoff booked for the following Monday. Deal closed-won in 42 days from first meeting.

The single deal produced: a scored ICP score, an accurate ACV data point, a reusable discovery script (with two questions that surfaced Ramesh's cycle-time KPI more efficiently than the previous version), a validated demo agenda, a validated proposal template, a proof point for the case-study library, and a next-step email chain worth reviewing. The deal is the unit of characterisation, not just the unit of revenue.

The Beta Data Platform deal, run in parallel, close-lost at Discovery on a disqualifier (Priya's team uses GitLab, not GitHub — the Layer-1 firmographic missed it because Beta's public presence looked like a GitHub shop). Close-lost is coded "unsupported code host," which rolls up to mod-104's ICP feedback and confirms the disqualifier is doing its job. The lost deal is characterisation too.

## Common failure patterns

- **The founder-doesn't-want-to-sell trap.** "I'm a builder, not a salesperson." The founder hires a VP Sales in month 4 without having personally closed anyone. The VP Sales, without a script, without ICP calibration, without pricing anchor, either burns 9 months producing nothing or produces revenue the founder cannot decode. Founders who avoid this trap start with mod-101's discovery interviews and treat the first sales calls as discovery interviews with a purchase-order attached — the discomfort recedes fast.
- **The stage-skipping trap.** A prospect asks for a demo on the first email reply; the founder schedules it without discovery. Every demo is a feature tour, the buyer says "let me think about it," and the deal never closes because no pain was ever sized. Chapters 3-4 fix this by making the discovery-to-demo gate explicit.
- **The pipeline-debt trap.** Every call ends with "let me follow up next week." Nothing is calendared. Weeks later 40 deals are "open" and none will close. Chapter 5's next-step discipline is the fix.
- **The forecast-lie trap.** Founder's forecast has 30 deals at 80% probability. She hits 8% of forecast at end of quarter. The pattern is un-diagnosable because there is no honest ICP-fit / stage / next-step data underneath. Chapter 7 fixes this.
- **The revenue-without-characterisation trap.** Founder closes 30 customers; three of them are churn risk within 90 days, and she cannot say which. The founder ran a sales motion but not a founder-led-sales motion — no pack, no repeatable script, no diagnostic pattern. Chapter 8's hand-off readiness check rejects a first sales hire against this baseline.
- **The pack-of-one artifact trap.** Founder writes a beautiful discovery script and stops. The demo is ad-hoc, the proposal is ad-hoc, the CRM is a spreadsheet with 47 columns. The first AE inherits one working instrument out of six and reinvents the rest from scratch. The point is the *complete* pack, not the best individual piece.
- **The premature-delegation trap.** Founder hires an SDR in month 2 to run outbound because she "doesn't have time." The SDR sends 500 emails against an unclear ICP, books 4 unqualified demos, and the founder concludes outbound doesn't work. The finding is un-decodable — bad SDR, bad script, bad ICP, or bad channel. Founder has to run outbound herself (Chapter 2) before delegating.
- **The wrong-frame trap.** Founder treats founder-led sales as adversarial "close the buyer" activity, applies aggressive-sales-book tactics, and burns the pipeline through a small ICP-fit community that talks. Founder-led sales in the modern B2B SaaS motion is *consultative*, not adversarial; the buyer is a partner in confirming or refuting the pain hypothesis, not a prey animal. Kazanjy is explicit on this point.

## Summary

- **Founding sales** is the founder-driven, customer-development-fused, artifact-producing motion that closes the first 20-30 customers and produces the pack of instruments a first AE can inherit. It is a distinct activity from career-AE sales along four axes: objective (characterisation, not quota), authority (unbounded, with risks), time budget (partial), feedback loop (into product / ICP / pricing / positioning).
- **Kazanjy's six-stage model** — prospect → outreach → discovery → demo → proposal → close, with next-step discipline as the connective tissue — is the scaffold for the whole module. Every open opportunity lives in exactly one stage; the stage model is enforced through the CRM (Chapter 7).
- The module produces a **three-tier pack**: per-stage instruments (Tier 1: outreach sequence, discovery script, demo checklist, proposal template, next-step emails), operating instruments (Tier 2: CRM stage definitions, weekly forecast, win/loss reasons), hand-off instruments (Tier 3: first-hire readiness, AE ramp, founder-out plan).
- **Four gates** break most founder-led-sales motions: prospect / outreach (never disciplined), discovery-to-demo (skipped), next-step (ended "let me follow up"), close-and-forecast (dishonest). Each has a specific artifact in the pack that fixes it.
- The pack has upstream dependencies: **mod-101 discovery** (pain shape), **mod-102 PMF** (pull vs. polite-interest), **mod-103 positioning** (shelf and narrative), **mod-104 ICP** (in / out scoring). Founding sales run without these produces closed-won revenue without characterisation.
- The **20-30 customer** target is the empirical band where the win-loss pattern, discovery script, and ACV distribution stabilise enough for the first AE to inherit them. Adjacent categories (very low or very high ACV) shift the number; Chapter 8's diagnostic uses the *pattern shape*, not the count, as hand-off trigger.
- Chapters 2-8 develop each stage of the pack individually. Chapter 2 opens the front of the funnel; Chapter 8 closes with the hand-off. The module's cumulative output is the pack, ready to hand to the first sales hire (mod-110).
