# Exercise 02 — Discovery Call Script and Role-Play

**Estimated time:** 3 hours
**Chapter link:** [`03-discovery-call-spiced-and-meddic.md`](../03-discovery-call-spiced-and-meddic.md)
**Prerequisite:** Chapter 3 read end-to-end; mod-104 ICP scorecard; Exercise 01 (cold outreach sequence — the accounts you sequenced are the discovery-call candidates).

## Problem statement

Chapter 3 named the discovery call as the single most-consequential 30 minutes in the six-stage model — where the ICP scorecard (mod-104) is applied in motion, where the value hypothesis from the outreach sequence (Chapter 2) is tested against the buyer's actual language, where the founder either qualifies the deal in or out with a defensible verdict and a calendared next step. Chapter 3 also named the failure mode: ad-hoc discovery, no written script, no disqualifier hooks — a motion where every call is different, every verdict is intuitive, no repeatable pattern can be handed to a first AE.

This exercise trains you to **author a written 8-12 question SPICED discovery script** (upgradeable to MEDDIC when ACV crosses the SMB threshold — Exercise 03 develops that upgrade), rehearse it in a **live role-play** against a peer or advisor playing the buyer, and produce a **post-call artifact set** (three-notes writeup + calendared next-step + reason-code-if-lost) that shows the discipline has landed. The output is a script the founder holds on-screen during real calls and a rehearsed delivery that does not read as an interrogation.

The failure mode this exercise exists to catch: **the founder authors a beautiful 25-question script, reads it verbatim on a role-play, the peer/advisor buyer disengages by minute 10 because the call feels like a checkbox exercise, and the founder concludes "discovery doesn't work at my ACV." What actually failed was the delivery, not the script — and the delivery only lands after rehearsal.**

## Requirements

Deliver a folder `exercise-02/` with:

- `part-a-discovery-script.md` — the written 8-12 question SPICED script, with opening frame, discovery arc, and verdict + next-step block.
- `part-b-role-play-transcript.md` — the recorded or transcribed role-play, with post-call debrief.
- `part-c-three-notes-and-next-step.md` — the founder's post-call artifact set.

### Part A — Author the discovery script (75 min)

Deliver `part-a-discovery-script.md`. Follow Chapter 3's three-section structure.

**A1 — Opening frame (10 min).** Draft a 2-3 sentence opening that contracts the call. Explicit content: what your company does (one sentence), the value hypothesis from the outreach that landed this meeting, the frame ("I'd like to spend most of this call understanding *your* situation and pain, then walk through whether we're a fit, and if we are — book a proper demo with your team"). The frame is what earns permission to ask questions.

**A2 — The 8-12 question SPICED arc (45 min).** Author the specific questions across the five SPICED letters. For each letter, deliver 1-3 questions with a specific *purpose* and a *listening cue* (what a "yes, that's us" answer sounds like vs. what a disqualifier answer sounds like).

Suggested distribution:

- **Situation (2 questions).** Team size / stack / current workflow. Low-stakes, gets the buyer talking.
- **Pain (2-3 questions).** Specific pain in the problem area. Open-ended; the "walk me through the last time you saw this pattern" question is often the strongest.
- **Impact (2-3 questions).** The sizing arithmetic. Include the specific *convert-to-dollars* question, the *convert-to-goals-missed* question, and (optionally) the *opportunity-cost* question. All open-ended.
- **Critical event (1-2 questions).** The specific event that forces the decision by a date. If the buyer cannot name one, discovery should flag it as a `NO_URGENCY` risk.
- **Decision (1-2 questions).** Names — not roles — for champion, buyer, and (per ACV) economic buyer. Ask the "any blockers?" question explicitly to surface the fifth persona (security, legal, incumbent-solution owner).

For each question, format as:

```
Q{n}. {SPICED letter}: {question, verbatim}
  Purpose: {what this question is diagnosing}
  Positive signal: {what a 'yes, this is us' answer sounds like}
  Disqualifier signal: {what a close-lost answer sounds like — cite the mod-104 disqualifier archetype}
  Follow-up if soft: {a probing follow-up if the buyer's answer is vague}
```

**A3 — Verdict + next step block (20 min).** Draft the 5-7 minute call closer that produces one of Chapter 3's four verdicts:

- Qualified in → book demo now (script the calendar-pull moment: "Tuesday 2pm or Thursday 11am — which works?").
- Qualified in but needs second call → book the specific follow-up with the specific missing element named.
- Needs-second-call with missing person / artifact → script the ask.
- Qualified out → script the honest disqualification (name the reason, refer or defer where legitimate, code the CRM reason).

Include the specific *language* for each verdict. Under pressure, having pre-loaded language is what enables the discipline; ad-libbing degrades every time.

### Part B — Run the role-play (60 min)

Deliver `part-b-role-play-transcript.md`. Find a peer, advisor, co-founder, or friend to play a buyer. Ideally the peer has some domain knowledge; ideally the peer has *not* read your script.

**Setup (5 min).** Give the peer a one-paragraph brief: they play the buyer for one of your Exercise-01 accounts. Give them a persona (role, seniority, company shape) and a rough pain profile — enough to react in character, not so much that they know all your questions in advance. Ask them to be occasionally-resistant (silent for 5 seconds when you ask something uncomfortable; deflect when asked about budget; feign not-knowing on the critical-event question) to stress-test the script.

**Run the call (25-30 min).** Record if possible (audio only is fine). Run the full SPICED arc + verdict + next-step. Use the script on-screen but do not read verbatim; hold it as a coverage list.

**Post-call debrief with the peer (15 min).** Ask the peer three questions:

- Did the call feel like a conversation or an interrogation? Where specifically?
- What surprised you (either good — the founder asked something you did not expect and it landed; or bad — the founder pushed on something that felt clumsy)?
- If you were a real buyer, would you have booked the next step? What would have improved the odds?

**Self-debrief (10 min).** Write down:

- The three questions on the script that landed cleanest.
- The three questions that felt awkward in delivery or produced weak answers.
- The moment the peer resisted (silence, deflection, "let me check with my team") and how you handled it — did you probe, or did you accept the deflection?
- The verdict you delivered on the call. Was it defensible against the coverage you got?

Transcribe the salient exchanges — full transcription is not required; capture the moments that inform the debrief.

### Part C — The three-notes writeup (15 min)

Deliver `part-c-three-notes-and-next-step.md`. Within 2 hours of the role-play (mirror the real-call discipline), write Chapter 3's three post-call notes:

1. **New language the buyer used** — words for the pain that differ from your current script or value hypothesis. Even in a role-play, the peer will use language that surprises you; capture it.
2. **The surprise** — anything the peer said that you did not expect. Surprises are the highest-signal input to iterating the script.
3. **The next-step commitment** — what was agreed on the call, with the date, exactly as you would send it in the confirming email (Chapter 5).

Also include:

- **Two specific script edits** owed for v1.1 — questions to sharpen, questions to add, questions to cut.
- **The reason-code** you would stamp if this deal closed today (from Chapter 7's list) — even if the deal is not closed, name the *provisional* reason so the pattern-analysis muscle is exercised.

## Starter guidance

- **Write the script before the role-play.** The temptation is to run the role-play first "to see what feels natural" and then reverse-engineer the script. This produces a script that codifies your existing tacit knowledge instead of stress-testing it. Author the script cold, run the role-play against it, iterate afterwards.
- **8-12 questions, not 47.** More questions turn the call into an interrogation. Force yourself to cut the questions that do not diagnose the SPICED letter cleanly. If you cannot name what a question is diagnosing, cut it.
- **Every question has a positive signal AND a disqualifier signal.** The disqualifier signal is what makes discovery a *filter* rather than a fact-finding survey. Author both explicitly.
- **The Impact section is where founders under-invest.** Founders default to Situation and Pain (comfortable) and skip Impact (uncomfortable — asking about dollars feels intrusive). Force yourself to author 2-3 real Impact questions and rehearse them until they feel conversational.
- **The Decision section is where founders default to roles instead of names.** Author questions that surface specific human names, not "your VP." "Who specifically owns the buying decision here, and what does their calendar look like this month?" is a name-forcing question; "Who's the decision-maker?" is not.
- **The peer for the role-play does not need to be a real buyer.** A co-founder, an advisor, or a friend playing the role is fine — the value is stress-testing the delivery, not simulating a real prospect. Ask them to be moderately resistant; a passive peer produces a false-positive rehearsal.
- **Record if you can, transcribe if you cannot.** Listening back to a role-play (even 10 minutes of it) reveals delivery patterns — long-winded questions, filling silence, pivoting too fast — that self-observation misses in the moment.
- **The verdict has to be delivered on the role-play.** Even if the "buyer" is a peer, close the call with the specific verdict language. Skipping the verdict is skipping the discipline the exercise exists to build.
- **The three notes are the founder's discipline, not the AE's.** Even in role-play, write them within 2 hours. The muscle memory is what makes founder-led discovery worth doing directly.

## Acceptance criteria

Your submission is complete when:

- [ ] Part A contains a written opening frame (2-3 sentences), 8-12 SPICED questions across all five letters, and a verdict + next-step block with pre-loaded language.
- [ ] Every question in Part A has an explicit `Purpose`, `Positive signal`, `Disqualifier signal`, and `Follow-up if soft`.
- [ ] Impact section contains at least 2 questions and includes a specific dollar-conversion question.
- [ ] Decision section includes an explicit "any blockers?" question and forces specific-name answers, not role-level answers.
- [ ] Part B contains a role-play transcript (full or salient-exchanges) with a real peer / advisor playing the buyer.
- [ ] Part B includes the peer's post-call debrief with the three specific questions asked.
- [ ] Part B includes the founder's self-debrief with named clean-landing questions, awkward questions, resistance moments, and verdict defensibility.
- [ ] Part C contains the three-notes writeup within the 2-hour rule.
- [ ] Part C names at least 2 specific script edits owed for v1.1.
- [ ] Part C names the provisional reason-code from Chapter 7's list.
- [ ] The role-play produced a defensible verdict (qualified in / needs-second / qualified out) — no "let me follow up next week" fallback.

## Common ways this exercise goes wrong

- **The 47-question script trap.** Founder authors a comprehensive coverage checklist. Role-play devolves into interrogation. Fix: 8-12 questions, cut anything that does not diagnose a SPICED letter cleanly.
- **The no-disqualifier-signal trap.** Every question has a positive signal but not a disqualifier signal. The script does not filter; every call comes back "qualified in." Fix: explicit disqualifier per question, traced to a mod-104 archetype.
- **The Impact-skipped trap.** Founder authors Situation and Pain thoroughly and hand-waves Impact. Deals open without fundable pain sizing. Fix: 2-3 real Impact questions rehearsed until they feel conversational.
- **The role-not-name Decision trap.** Decision questions ask for roles ("who's the decision-maker?"). Fix: name-forcing questions ("who specifically owns this decision, and what does their calendar look like?").
- **The peer-is-too-easy trap.** Role-play peer plays a friendly, informative buyer who answers every question cleanly. Founder concludes the script works. Fix: brief the peer to be occasionally-resistant (silence, deflection, "let me check").
- **The no-verdict-on-call trap.** Founder ends the role-play with "I'll follow up." The exercise's whole point is skipped. Fix: script the verdict language and deliver it on every role-play.
- **The read-verbatim trap.** Founder reads the script word-for-word on the role-play. Every question sounds robotic. Fix: hold the script as a coverage list, deliver conversationally.
- **The no-recording trap.** Founder relies on memory for the self-debrief. Delivery patterns invisible. Fix: audio-record the role-play if the peer consents; listen back.
- **The three-notes-skipped trap.** Founder is exhausted post-call, skips the three notes. The customer-development half of discovery is lost. Fix: 2-hour rule; write the notes even if they are rough.
- **The no-script-edits trap.** Founder role-plays, feels good, ships the script unchanged. No iteration signal captured. Fix: at least 2 script edits every role-play — every role-play produces v-next.
- **The provisional-reason-code-skipped trap.** Founder skips the reason-code exercise because "it's just a role-play." Pattern-analysis muscle atrophies. Fix: stamp the provisional code; the muscle memory transfers to real deals.
- **The soft-follow-up trap in role-play.** Peer resists with "let me check my team"; founder accepts the deflection instead of probing. Real deals go the same way. Fix: probe honestly ("What would you need from me to make the case?") — the resistance is a signal, not a wall.
- **The founder-monologue trap.** Founder talks 70% of the call, buyer talks 30%. Discovery inverted. Fix: after every question, shut up for a full 60-120 seconds; the peer will fill the silence.
