# Cold Outreach and Top-of-Funnel by Hand

## Motivation

Nothing in the six-stage model runs if the top of the funnel is empty. And at SEED, the top of the funnel is almost never full of inbound — inbound requires the mod-108 content / community / SEO investment to have been running for months, and mod-108 is downstream of this module for a reason. The founder has to *manufacture* the first-meeting pipeline by hand, one account at a time, through cold outreach — cold email, cold calls, warm intros through the founder network, and targeted social outreach on LinkedIn or the platform the ICP actually reads.

Founding-sales outbound is not the same activity as scaled outbound. The scaled outbound motion — 100 SDRs, 500-account sequences, template-per-persona — is what mod-107 designs later. This chapter is about the founder-driven, ICP-scored, single-digit-daily-volume outreach that gets the first 20-30 first meetings on the calendar. The bar is different: the founder is doing 5-15 heavily-personalised touches per day, not 500 templated ones per week, and every reply is a data point that feeds back into the ICP, the positioning, and the discovery script.

The failure mode this chapter exists to catch: **the founder either sends 200 identical personalised-looking emails in a single sitting, gets 3 replies, concludes "outbound doesn't work," and pivots to waiting for inbound; or hires an SDR to run outbound in month 2 before the founder has personally validated a sequence that produces reply-rates within reasonable range, and burns the SDR's ramp on a script that was never characterised.** Both patterns kill the top of the funnel and leave the six-stage motion running on empty.

The remedy: the founder runs a small, disciplined, measured outbound sequence — 15-30 accounts per week, 4-6 touches per account over 2-3 weeks, hand-personalised, tracked in a spreadsheet — and iterates the sequence against reply rate, positive-reply rate, and meetings-booked. The output is a validated sequence the first AE / SDR can inherit, plus a working intuition for which channels (email, LinkedIn, warm intro, hand-written mail, in-person conference) produce the strongest response for this specific ICP.

## Core concepts

### The three-channel top-of-funnel

Founder-driven top-of-funnel at SEED runs on three channels simultaneously, with different mixes for different ICPs.

1. **Cold email.** The bread-and-butter channel for B2B SaaS mid-market and enterprise. Scales in effort (a good sequence can be run against 20-50 accounts per week by a founder), has legible reply-rate math, produces artifacts (subject lines, opening lines, CTAs) that ship into the first-AE pack.
2. **LinkedIn (or the platform-of-the-ICP).** The complement to cold email — connection request + follow-up message, comment-then-message, InMail. Higher personalisation ceiling, lower message throughput, useful for accounts where the founder has a real second-degree connection. For non-B2B-SaaS ICPs the equivalent platform is Twitter/X (dev-tooling), Reddit or Discord (community-driven), TikTok (consumer-adjacent) — the channel is where the buyer *reads*, not where "everyone does outreach."
3. **Warm intros.** Highest reply-rate channel by far, lowest volume. The founder's network — investors, advisors, prior colleagues, portfolio-peer founders, YC / Techstars / accelerator peers — is a first-round outbound channel that most founders under-use because it feels awkward. It should not; it is the highest-ROI channel available at SEED and every founder should spend one hour a week specifically curating and running it.

The three-channel mix is not either/or; a well-run founder-led outbound motion runs all three, layered per account. An account that got a cold email at t+0 and a LinkedIn connection request at t+3 and a warm-intro nudge from a mutual investor at t+7 has three-times the surface area of any single channel used alone.

### Cold email — the sequence, not the single email

A single cold email at the founding-sales scale is expected to have a low reply rate — a benchmark commonly-cited across B2B SaaS practitioners is **1-5% positive-reply rate on a well-targeted, well-written first touch**, with substantial variance by ICP, industry, sender reputation, and category. <!-- needs-research: pin a specific benchmark and citation for founder-led cold-email positive-reply rate against B2B SaaS mid-market ICPs from a primary source (Outreach.io report, Predictable Revenue publication, or SaaStr / OpenView blog with methodology) -->

The scaling factor is not the single email — it is the **sequence**. A well-run 4-6 touch sequence over 2-3 weeks compounds the reply rate; empirically, the *cumulative* positive-reply rate on a full sequence lands multiple times higher than the first touch alone, because different buyers respond to different angles, and some buyers respond only to the break-up or the third touch.

A canonical founder-scale sequence shape:

- **Touch 1 (day 0)** — hand-personalised opener, one specific value hypothesis, one specific soft ask. 3-5 sentences. Subject line tied to the personalisation, not to the product.
- **Touch 2 (day 3-4)** — same thread, "did this land?" plus one *different* angle (a different pain the buyer might feel, or a specific customer proof-point relevant to their segment).
- **Touch 3 (day 7-8)** — cross-channel: LinkedIn connection request or InMail referencing the email chain; or a warm-intro nudge if a mutual is identified.
- **Touch 4 (day 12-14)** — email touch back, third angle (a case-study snippet, a piece of content, a shift in the market that changes their pain calculus).
- **Touch 5 (day 18-20)** — the break-up: "assuming this isn't a priority right now — mind if I close the loop?" Counter-intuitively, break-ups often produce the highest reply rate in the whole sequence because they force the buyer to react to the pending close.
- **Touch 6 (optional, day 30+)** — reactivation with a genuinely new hook: a product update, a new case study, a market event. Not a repeat of the first sequence.

Each touch has a specific *ask*. The specific ask is the mechanic that separates founder-scale outbound from spray-and-pray outbound. The ask escalates gradually: touch 1 asks for a specific question ("does this pain show up on your team?"), touch 2 asks for a 15-minute call, touch 4 asks for a 30-minute meeting, touch 5 asks for permission to close the loop.

### The personalisation-vs-scale trade-off

The naive framing is "personalise every email, or send templates at scale." The founder-scale motion sits in a specific middle: **the personalisation is in the opening; the value hypothesis is templated; the ask is templated.** Every email in a founder-scale sequence has a 2-4-sentence hand-authored opener that references the specific account (a recent product launch, a hiring pattern, a strategic statement from the CEO's public writing, a specific piece of infrastructure the account uses), followed by a 3-5-sentence templated value hypothesis + ask.

The rule of thumb: **if the founder cannot show a 3-5-sentence line of personalisation on every touch 1, the volume is too high**. The volume ceiling for a founder is 15-30 hand-personalised touch-1's per week (2-4 hours of research + writing), which at a 5-15% first-meeting-per-account rate produces 2-5 first meetings per week — the right scale for a founder-led motion.

Templates without personalisation collapse to spam. Personalisation without a repeatable value hypothesis is unscalable and unteachable. Founder-scale outbound is the specific combination.

### The value hypothesis, not the product pitch

Cold emails that describe the product fail. Cold emails that describe a *specific pain the buyer will recognise* and a *specific outcome addressing that pain* succeed. The value hypothesis is the compression of the mod-101 discovery + mod-103 positioning + mod-104 persona work into a 2-3-sentence articulation the buyer can react to.

Anatomy of a working value hypothesis:

- **Named pain**: "when your PR review times drift past two days, deploy velocity halves and your on-call rotation gets angrier." Not "PR review is important." The named pain has to be a pain the buyer's persona has articulated to peers — the language should sound like the ICP's own, not like a copywriter's.
- **Named consequence**: "the cycle-time KPI ends up on the VP Eng scorecard, and the fix — chasing engineers on Slack — is not scalable." The consequence is what the buyer's peers, boss, or KPI-owner would say the pain *costs*.
- **Named outcome (one line)**: "we tighten cycle time by 20-40% by automating the chase — engineering managers get their evenings back." Not a feature description; an outcome sentence.
- **Named specificity**: at least one number, one named integration, or one named comparable customer. "Works with GitHub in ~15 minutes to set up" is stronger than "easy setup." "Loom, Retool, and Vercel use it" — if those are real customers — is stronger than "trusted by leading dev tools companies."

The value hypothesis is not the tagline; it is the *reason to reply*. If the recipient reads the two-sentence hypothesis and cannot state, in her own words, "the ask is X and it maps to a pain I have," the hypothesis is not landing.

### The two-metric outbound scoreboard

Founder-scale outbound is measured by two metrics, both tracked in a spreadsheet, both reviewed weekly:

- **Reply rate** (any reply, positive or negative). Low reply rate (< 5% on a well-targeted sequence over 4-6 touches) means the opener + subject line + sender profile is not landing. The fix is in the touch-1 personalisation and the sender's LinkedIn profile / email signature / domain deliverability.
- **Positive-reply rate** (replies that agree to a call, want more info, or refer internally). Positive-reply rate is what actually generates meetings. Low positive-reply rate with high reply rate ("thanks but not now" replies) means the value hypothesis is not landing — the pain is not perceived or the outcome does not match.

The two metrics diagnose two different problems: opener problem (fix personalisation) vs. hypothesis problem (fix value articulation). Founders who track only "reply rate" flatten the two problems into one and iterate randomly. Founders who track both know exactly which lever to pull.

A third downstream metric — **first-meetings-per-100-accounts** — is the ultimate output, but the leading indicators (reply-rate + positive-reply-rate) are what let the founder iterate the sequence week over week without waiting three weeks for a meeting cohort to close.

### Cold calls — the reintroduction

Cold calling was left for dead in the 2010s and has quietly returned as a competitive advantage in categories with high cold-email fatigue. For certain ICPs — sales leadership, RevOps, non-technical execs — cold calling has a higher first-meeting-per-touch rate than cold email, precisely because most competitors have abandoned it.

Founder-scale cold calling: 15-30 dials per week to named accounts already in an email sequence, timed to the day *after* an email touch (so the touch is warm-ish), with a scripted 30-second opener that references the email and asks for a 5-minute conversation-to-set-up-a-longer-meeting rather than a 20-minute sales call. Cold calling is uncomfortable and pays off; the founders who run it disproportionately book the meetings competitors cannot.

For technical / developer-tool ICPs, cold calling is often a negative-return channel — the buyer's culture actively rejects phone calls and rewards asynchronous, thoughtful text. The ICP determines whether cold calling is on the channel mix; the founder should test 30 dials before deciding.

### Warm intros — the one hour a week most founders skip

Every founder has an underweighted asset: her *own network*. Investors, advisors, YC / accelerator peers, prior colleagues, portfolio-peer founders, former customers from her prior company. Each of these is a potential warm-intro source for one or more ICP accounts.

The one-hour-a-week discipline:

1. Pick 5-10 target ICP accounts for the coming week.
2. For each, ask: who in my network is one degree from someone at this account? (LinkedIn's second-degree search + the founder's own memory of who works where.)
3. For each account with a mutual, draft a **forwardable email** — the founder writes a 3-4-sentence "here's what I'd love an intro to, here's the ask, here's why the buyer would want to talk to me" that the mutual can literally forward without editing.
4. Send the forwardable to the mutual with a specific ask: "would you feel comfortable forwarding this to X?"
5. Track the intro requests in the outbound spreadsheet.

Warm-intro **positive-reply rates** run dramatically higher than cold — often 30-60% first-meeting-per-forwarded-intro when the mutual is credibly connected and the ask is well-scoped. The channel is limited by (a) the founder's actual network breadth and (b) the founder's willingness to ask for help — the latter is by far the more common constraint. A founder who is uncomfortable asking her lead investor for two intros a month is leaving the highest-ROI channel on the table.

### The subject-line and opening-line discipline

Two lines carry a disproportionate share of the whole sequence's outcome: **the subject line** (which drives open) and **the opening line** (which drives read-through).

- **Subject line rules of thumb.** Short (3-6 words). Specific — references the account, a mutual, or a specific artifact ("your Q3 platform launch"), not the product name. Lowercase and non-marketing-looking (matches how the buyer's own colleagues would write). No emojis, no exclamation marks, no "quick question" (dead pattern). Ideally under 40 characters so it renders in the mobile preview pane.
- **Opening-line rules of thumb.** The opening line must not be a compliment ("love what you're building") — this is spam-signature. It should be a *specific observation* that could only apply to this account: "noticed you shipped the new query planner in October — congrats, that's a meaningful ship." The observation earns the next sentence; the next sentence introduces the value hypothesis.

These are the two lines the founder authors by hand for every touch-1. The templated middle and ask are downstream of these two lines landing.

### Sender reputation and deliverability

Cold email that lands in spam has a 0% reply rate. Founder-scale outbound has to pay attention to sender reputation — the technical layer that determines whether the email arrives in the inbox at all.

- **Warm the sending domain.** A brand-new domain sending 20 emails a day looks like spam to Google and Microsoft filters. Warm-up services (Warmup Inbox, Instantly, MailReach, similar) simulate legitimate inbox behaviour for 2-6 weeks before real sending scales up. <!-- needs-research: confirm a specific well-regarded warm-up service and typical warm-up duration for a new domain running founder-led-sales outbound -->
- **SPF, DKIM, DMARC.** The three DNS records that let receiving servers verify the sender is legitimate. All three configured correctly is table stakes for founder-scale outbound; missing DMARC in particular causes deliverability collapses.
- **Send from a secondary domain.** A common pattern: buy `getproductname.com` as the outbound sending domain, use `productname.com` as the primary product / marketing domain. The two are visually similar and the practice protects the primary domain's reputation from any outbound spam-flag events. Not universally required, but a common founder-scale pattern.
- **Volume ramp.** Do not send 200 emails on day 1 from a new domain. Ramp 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 over the first two weeks. Founder-scale volume (15-30 per day) is naturally in-band; the risk is running an outbound tool that batches to 300 in a single burst.
- **Reply-tracking, not open-tracking.** Open tracking (invisible pixel in the email) has become a spam signal — Google and Microsoft filters weight tracked emails more suspiciously. Founder-scale outbound tracks *replies*, not opens; the metric that matters is the reply anyway.

Deliverability is boring plumbing that determines whether the outbound sequence has a chance to work. Founders who skip it produce data that says "outbound doesn't work" when in fact 80% of their emails never reached the inbox.

## Concrete example — Loomly's month-4 founder outbound sequence

Loomly's founder Jane runs a 4-touch cold email + LinkedIn sequence against 20 mid-market devtools accounts per week (80 per month). Sender is `jane@getloomly.com` (secondary sending domain), warmed for 3 weeks, SPF/DKIM/DMARC in place.

Touch 1 example (personalised for Acme Observability, VP Eng Ramesh):

> Subject: Acme's Q3 tracing ship
>
> Ramesh — noticed Acme shipped the new tracing pipeline in October; that's a meaningful ship. Two things I saw from your engineering blog: distributed team, and Sasha's talk at StaffPlusCon about your throughput problem.
>
> When PR review times drift past two days, deploy velocity halves and the cycle-time metric your VP reports on drifts hard. We built Loomly specifically for the "chase stalled PRs" problem — 50-150-engineer teams on GitHub tighten cycle time 20-40% within 6 weeks; three of our current customers look a lot like Acme (Loom, Retool, Vercel).
>
> Worth a 15-minute conversation to see whether this shape maps to what Sasha is dealing with?
>
> — Jane

Touch 2 (day 4) reuses the thread, adds a specific proof point: "one thing I forgot to mention — Sasha's talk mentioned 'engineer-owned throughput'; our GitHub app surfaces this to the manager without a Slackbot. Happy to walk through the shape in 15 minutes if useful." Touch 3 (day 8) is a LinkedIn connection request from Jane to Ramesh with a 1-line note. Touch 4 (day 14) is the break-up: "assuming Q4 planning is in your way — mind if I close the loop until Q1?"

Weekly scoreboard after month 4:

- 80 accounts sequenced, 4-touch each.
- Reply rate: 21% (17 replies of 80).
- Positive-reply rate: 6% (5 of 80 → wanting a call).
- First meetings booked: 4 (one converted from LinkedIn touch, one from break-up, two from touch 1-2 replies).
- Sequence-level meetings-per-100-accounts: 5.

Loomly's founder tracks these three metrics weekly. Two months earlier the reply rate was 8% and the positive-reply rate was 1%; iterating the opener personalisation lifted reply rate, iterating the value hypothesis lifted positive-reply rate. The sequence is now stable enough to hand to the first SDR when the pack is ready (Chapter 8).

The 5% meetings-per-100-accounts is founder-scale, not scaled-motion, math. When Loomly hires an SDR at Series A and runs 500 accounts a month with less personalisation, the meeting rate will drop — but by then the sequence is validated at founder scale and the drop-off is a known trade-off, not an unknown collapse.

## Common failure patterns

- **The 200-emails-in-a-batch trap.** Founder writes one great email, blasts to 200 accounts on Monday, gets 3 replies, gives up. The math never had a chance to work; the 200-account single-touch has a lower reply rate than a 20-account 4-touch. Fix: replace batch-blast with sequenced touches against fewer accounts.
- **The templated-but-look-personalised trap.** Emails that use `{{firstName}}` and `{{company}}` and no actual research land as spam. Buyers detect fake personalisation faster than founders think. Fix: 2-4 hand-written sentences per touch-1 that reference something real about the account.
- **The product-pitch-in-touch-1 trap.** The email describes the product's features, not the buyer's pain. The buyer reads a marketing paragraph and archives. Fix: value hypothesis, not product pitch — named pain → named consequence → named outcome, in that order.
- **The over-cited-social-proof trap.** "Trusted by leading companies including X, Y, Z" when X, Y, Z are not real customers. Buyers verify; the trust cost of a fake proof point is bigger than the persuasion gain. Fix: cite only real, relevant, verifiable customers.
- **The one-channel-only trap.** Founder runs email-only against an ICP that reads Twitter and skips LinkedIn. Zero reply rate is not "outbound doesn't work"; it is wrong-channel. Fix: match channel to ICP's reading platform, not to founder comfort.
- **The no-warm-intros trap.** Founder never asks her lead investor, her advisors, or her YC batch for intros. The highest-ROI channel goes unused. Fix: one hour per week, 5-10 forwardable intros to mutual connections.
- **The no-break-up-touch trap.** Sequence ends politely at touch 3 without asking permission to close the loop. Founder misses the 5-15% of buyers who respond only to the break-up because it forces a decision. Fix: always include the break-up.
- **The no-scoreboard trap.** Founder runs outbound without tracking reply rate + positive-reply rate weekly. Iteration is random and slow. Fix: spreadsheet, weekly review, two metrics per sequence variant.
- **The deliverability-blind trap.** Founder sends from `jane@loomly.com` without SPF/DKIM/DMARC or domain warm-up. Half the emails go to spam. Founder concludes outbound doesn't work; in fact, the emails were never seen. Fix: technical setup as table stakes.
- **The SDR-before-founder trap.** Founder hires an SDR in month 2 to run outbound before running it herself. SDR runs an un-characterised script; results are un-diagnosable. Fix: founder runs outbound long enough to validate a sequence (typically 60-90 days) before hiring an SDR to run it.
- **The channel-fatigue trap.** Founder runs the same subject line and opener for 6 months; reply rate degrades. Buyers in a tight ICP community talk. Fix: refresh the opener and hypothesis every 6-8 weeks; keep the core sequence, evolve the specifics.
- **The wrong-time-horizon trap.** Founder sends 20 emails on Monday and expects meetings on Wednesday. A 4-touch sequence is 2-3 weeks; the meetings from Monday's sequence arrive weeks 3-4. Founder gives up in week 2. Fix: patience with the sequence math; only judge after a full sequence cycle.

## Summary

- Founder-scale top-of-funnel runs on **three channels** — cold email, LinkedIn / platform-of-the-ICP, warm intros — layered per account. The mix is ICP-dependent; the founder tests to find what lands.
- The unit of measurement is the **sequence** (4-6 touches over 2-3 weeks), not the single email. First-touch reply rates are low by design; sequenced touches compound.
- Personalisation lives in the **opening 2-4 sentences** (hand-authored per account); the value hypothesis and the ask are templated. Volume ceiling at founder scale is 15-30 hand-personalised touch-1's per week.
- The **value hypothesis** — named pain, named consequence, named outcome, named specificity — replaces the product pitch. If the buyer cannot restate the ask in her own words, the hypothesis is not landing.
- Two metrics run the outbound scoreboard weekly: **reply rate** (opener quality) and **positive-reply rate** (hypothesis quality). Tracking both distinguishes opener problems from hypothesis problems.
- **Cold calling** has quietly returned as a competitive advantage for non-developer ICPs where competitors have abandoned it; 15-30 dials per week to sequenced accounts is founder-scale.
- **Warm intros** are the highest-ROI channel and the most underused. One hour per week producing 5-10 forwardable intros to mutuals is the discipline.
- The **subject line and opening line** carry the sequence — short, specific, non-marketing-looking subject; specific observation (never a compliment) in the opener.
- **Deliverability plumbing** (domain warm-up, SPF/DKIM/DMARC, reply-tracking not open-tracking, secondary sending domain) is boring and determines whether the sequence has a chance to run. Skipping it produces "outbound doesn't work" that is really "outbound never landed."
- Founder runs outbound herself **before** hiring an SDR. The pack (Exercise 01) is the validated sequence + reply-rate math + channel mix that the first SDR / AE inherits. Delegating before validating produces un-diagnosable outcomes.
- Chapter 3 picks up the funnel at the **first meeting booked** — the discovery call the outbound sequence exists to produce.
