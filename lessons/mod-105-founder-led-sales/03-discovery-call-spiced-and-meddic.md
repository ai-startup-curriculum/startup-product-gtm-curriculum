# The Discovery Call — SPICED, MEDDIC, and MEDDPICC

## Motivation

The discovery call is the single most-consequential 30 minutes in the six-stage model. It is where the founder finds out whether the account has the pain the product addresses, sizes that pain against dollars or hours, identifies the actual buyer (and the actual champion, and the actual user), gates the deal in or out with a defensible verdict, and — critically — leaves the call with a calendared next step or an explicit disqualification. Discovery is where the ICP scorecard (mod-104) gets applied in motion; it is where the value hypothesis (Chapter 2) gets tested against the buyer's actual language; and it is where the founder learns whether the deal she is about to invest 15-30 hours in is a real deal or an expensively-lost one.

Discovery is also the stage founder-led sales most-often skips. A prospect replies to a cold email with "sure, can you demo?" and the founder — flattered by the interest, cautious of "over-qualifying" — jumps straight to a demo. The demo becomes a feature tour, no pain is sized, no buyer is identified, no next step is defensible, and the deal enters the pipeline as an open opportunity that will never close. The founder concludes the buyer is a bad fit; the reality is that no discovery was ever run.

The failure mode this chapter exists to catch: **the founder runs discovery ad-hoc, without a framework, without a script, without disqualification hooks — so every call is different, every verdict is intuitive, and no repeatable pattern can be handed to the first AE hire.** Or worse: the founder runs discovery so aggressively (a 47-question checklist read from a spreadsheet) that the buyer feels interrogated, disengages, and the deal is lost on the first call not because the fit was poor but because the founder made the interaction extractive rather than consultative.

The remedy: run discovery through a *named framework* — SPICED for mid-market and below, MEDDIC or MEDDPICC when ACV moves above the SMB range — with a written script, a specific list of pain-sizing questions, a specific list of buyer-identification questions, and a specific disqualifier check. The framework is the scaffold; the founder's conversational skill is what makes it feel like a conversation instead of an interrogation. Both are required. Neither is optional.

## Core concepts

### What discovery is (and is not)

**Discovery** is the founder-led (later AE-led) conversation whose objective is to *find the pain, size the pain, identify the buyer, and gate the deal in or out with a calendared next step.* It is not a demo (which happens next), not a pitch (which happens embedded in the pain-sizing conversation), and not a fact-finding survey (which is the interrogation failure mode).

Three properties define a functioning discovery call:

- **Bidirectional.** The founder is asking questions to characterise the deal; the buyer is asking questions to characterise the vendor. Both are qualifying each other. A discovery call where only one side asks questions has failed one direction.
- **Consultative.** The founder is helping the buyer articulate a pain the buyer may not have fully framed on her own. The classic mode is "here's a pattern we've seen across teams at your stage — does it map to what you're seeing?" The framing is diagnostic, not extractive.
- **Verdict-producing.** The call has to end with a specific verdict: **qualified in with a booked demo**, **qualified in with a scheduled follow-up**, **needs-second-call with a specific missing person or artifact**, or **qualified out with a specific reason coded.** The verdict is the point of the call. If the founder ends the call without one — "let me follow up next week" — the call has failed regardless of how it felt.

### The SPICED framework — Winning by Design's discovery arc

**SPICED** is the discovery framework developed by Winning by Design (Jacco van der Kooij and team), designed for modern B2B SaaS mid-market and below. It is the discovery framework this module treats as the default. The five letters:

- **S — Situation.** The buyer's current state — team size, tech stack, current workflow for the problem area, current tools, org structure. Situation gets the founder oriented and gives the buyer a low-stakes place to start talking.
- **P — Pain.** The specific pain the buyer feels in the problem area. Not the founder's hypothesis of the pain; the buyer's articulation of it. Pain has to be *concrete* — a specific thing that has gone wrong, a specific frustration, a specific broken workflow — not "we're always looking to improve."
- **I — Impact.** The consequence of the pain in dollars, hours, headcount, missed goals, retention losses, or team morale. Impact is what turns a mild pain into a fundable pain. A pain worth $5K/year to fix will not close a $15K/year deal; a pain worth $200K/year to fix will.
- **C — Critical event.** The specific event that forces the buyer to make a decision *by a date*. "We're planning our Q4 goals in three weeks and cycle time has to be one of them." Without a critical event, the deal has no natural close date and drifts. Critical events can be internal (board meeting, planning cycle, hiring milestone) or external (regulatory deadline, contract renewal with a competitor, product launch).
- **D — Decision.** The decision-making process — the buyer, the champion, the economic buyer, the influencers, the procurement path, the paper process. Decision is where the deal architecture becomes visible; skipping it produces a deal that "close-losts because their VP hadn't been in the loop."

SPICED is a **flexible arc**, not a rigid checklist. A well-run discovery call weaves the five in whatever order the conversation goes — Pain might come before Situation, Impact might get re-visited after Decision surfaces a stakeholder who cares about a different KPI. The letters are the coverage list at the end of the call, not the linear script.

### Sizing pain — the impact conversation

Sizing pain is where most founders under-invest. "It's slowing us down" is not sized; "it's slowing us down by 30% on cycle time, which delayed our last release by two weeks, which pushed a customer's renewal into next quarter — call it $80K in delayed revenue" is sized. The second version is what makes the deal fundable; the first version is what makes the deal a "we're still evaluating."

The sizing questions have a rough shape:

- **Convert time to dollars.** "How many hours per week is the team losing to this? How many people? What are those people paid?" Even a rough per-hour number ($100/hour is a common blended-mid-market default; higher for senior specialised roles) produces a defensible cost.
- **Convert goals-missed to dollars.** "What was the team's target? What did you actually hit? What did the miss cost — a customer, a launch, a quarter's growth number?" Goal-attainment framing lets the buyer size against their existing scorecard.
- **Convert to opportunity cost.** "If you got 20% of this pain back, what would the team spend the time on?" Opportunity-cost framing turns the pain into a growth argument rather than a cost-avoidance argument, which usually funds larger deals.
- **Compare to alternatives.** "What have you tried already? What did that cost — hours to build, money to buy?" The buyer's existing spend on the problem is a hard floor for what she's willing to pay for a better solution.

The founder does not need to walk in with a spreadsheet. She needs to walk in with **the questions** and the willingness to work the arithmetic with the buyer in the moment. The exercise of doing the math *together* is itself part of the sale — the buyer emerges with a shared number she will use internally to justify the purchase.

### Identifying the buyer, champion, user, economic buyer

mod-104 defined the buyer / user / champion split at the persona level. Discovery is where the founder identifies the specific *people* at this account who occupy each role — and where a fourth role, the **economic buyer**, becomes explicit.

- **Champion** — the person at the account who wants the deal to happen and will work internally to make it happen. Usually the person who took the first meeting. Champions have to (a) genuinely want the outcome (not just be curious), (b) have credibility with the buyer, and (c) be willing to spend political capital selling the deal internally. Champions who fail any of the three are not champions; they are "interested contacts."
- **Buyer** — the person who owns the budget the deal will draw from. Sometimes the champion; often not. Discovery has to surface the buyer's name and the buyer's process for approval.
- **Economic buyer** — the person with veto authority over the spend. At small ACV in a mid-market deal, this is often the same as the buyer (a VP owns her own budget within a threshold). At larger ACV it is a level up — a CFO, a CEO, a departmental head. The economic buyer's involvement is what turns a proposal into a signed deal; the deal that doesn't identify the economic buyer often close-losts at the last step "because the CFO said no."
- **User** — the people who will use the product day-to-day. Users often gate deals through veto ("we tried it, the team hated the UX"); their involvement in the demo is often necessary. Users are also the primary source of expansion (mod-109), so discovery should surface who they are even if they're not on the first call.
- **Blockers** — a fifth role that Kazanjy calls out explicitly. The person at the account who has a reason to say no — a security lead, a competing vendor's champion, a legal advisor, an incumbent-solution owner. Blockers do not appear on org charts; they surface when the deal starts moving. Discovery should ask "who else needs to weigh in?" specifically to surface blockers before proposal.

The founder should exit discovery with names — not roles — for at least champion, buyer, and (where ACV requires) economic buyer. "Their VP Eng" is a role; "Ramesh Kumar, VP Eng, employee since 2022, KPI-owner for cycle time" is a name with context. Names are what the follow-up email addresses; roles are what pipeline reviews un-diagnosably reference.

### MEDDIC and MEDDPICC — the enterprise-level upgrades

**MEDDIC** is a discovery-and-qualification framework developed at PTC in the 1990s under sales leader Jack Napoli, subsequently popularised across enterprise B2B software. It is a stricter, higher-effort framework than SPICED, designed for larger ACV, longer sales cycles, and more-complex buying committees. <!-- needs-research: pin a primary source for MEDDIC's PTC origin — Jack Napoli's public writing, MEDDPICC.com organisation, or a well-cited secondary source -->

MEDDIC's letters:

- **M — Metrics.** The specific quantitative outcomes the buyer expects — the impact number from SPICED, but stricter: the exact number the buyer will use to justify the purchase internally. "Reduce cycle time by 30%, saving $200K/year in delayed revenue." Metrics are what the champion carries into the internal deal review.
- **E — Economic buyer.** Identified by name, engaged directly (ideally, meeting them once during the sales cycle), understood in terms of *their* KPIs and *their* decision criteria.
- **D — Decision criteria.** The stated criteria the buyer will use to compare vendors — technical requirements, integration needs, security posture, price bands. Decision criteria have to be surfaced and, where the product does not natively meet a criterion, either the criterion has to be re-negotiated or the deal is not a fit.
- **D — Decision process.** The specific sequence of steps from "we're interested" to "signed contract" — technical evaluation, security review, procurement, legal, countersign. Every step has an owner and an approximate duration. Skipping the decision-process conversation is how enterprise deals slip a quarter without anyone predicting it.
- **I — Identify pain.** The pain-sizing conversation (Impact from SPICED) — with more emphasis on multiple stakeholders' perceptions of pain. Enterprise deals almost always have multiple pain articulations across the buying committee; the pain across all of them has to be characterised.
- **C — Champion.** Identified by name, coached explicitly on how to sell the deal internally, given the materials she needs (the ROI number, the case studies, the internal talking points). Enterprise MEDDIC champions are trained assets, not passive contacts.

**MEDDPICC** adds two letters — **P — Paper process** (the specific contract, redline, and legal process — often the single longest stage of a large deal) and **C — Competition** (the incumbent solution, the alternative vendors, the "build it in-house" option — characterised specifically). MEDDPICC is the modern enterprise standard; MEDDIC is the older, still-taught, still-valid predecessor.

### When to use which framework

The framework depends on the ACV band and the deal complexity, roughly:

- **ACV < $10K, single-buyer, transactional cycle (< 4 weeks).** SPICED is enough; MEDDIC's Metrics + Economic-buyer + Decision-process discipline is over-engineering for a deal where the buyer buys with a credit card.
- **ACV $10K-$50K, small buying committee, 4-8 week cycle.** SPICED is the default. Layer in MEDDIC's Economic-buyer identification when the buyer references a budget approval up the chain.
- **ACV $50K-$250K, formal buying committee, 8-16 week cycle.** MEDDIC is the working default. Champion coaching (the "C") is where deals live or die; the founder invests real time enabling the champion.
- **ACV > $250K, formal procurement, 16+ week cycle.** MEDDPICC — the paper process and competition-mapping additions become load-bearing. Deals under-managed on Paper process slip a quarter; deals under-managed on Competition surface a competitor at the eleventh hour and lose.

Founders often start their motion at the low end (SPICED) and, if their ICP + pricing pushes them up the ACV band, upgrade the discovery framework as they go. The upgrade is not automatic; the founder has to consciously add MEDDIC discipline when the deal shape demands it, or the enterprise deals slip and no one can say why.

### The written discovery script — 8-12 questions, not 47

The discovery script is a *written*, *held-in-hand-or-on-screen*, 8-12-question working document. Not a mental model; not a founder-tacit-knowledge asset that lives in her head. A written script is what makes the motion transferrable (Chapter 8's hand-off depends on it).

The script has three sections:

1. **Opening frame** (2-3 minutes) — a specific one-paragraph setup: "here's what we do, here's the value hypothesis I sent, I'd like to spend most of this call understanding *your* situation and pain, then walk through whether we're a fit, and if we are — book a proper demo with your team." The frame contracts the call and gives the buyer a legitimate reason to answer questions.
2. **Discovery arc** (18-22 minutes) — the 8-12 questions across SPICED (or MEDDIC), asked conversationally, in whatever order the conversation flows. The founder has the list; she does not read from it. The list is her safety net for coverage.
3. **Verdict + next step** (5-7 minutes) — the founder summarises what she heard, states her assessment ("this looks like a strong fit / I'd like to think about whether we're the right fit / here's where I have concerns"), and either books the next step on the call or explicitly closes the loop as qualified-out with a reason.

The 8-12 question count is deliberate. Fewer than 8 usually means SPICED coverage is incomplete. More than 12 turns the call into an interrogation. Exercise 02 develops the specific question set.

### The next-step gate at the end of discovery

Every discovery call exits through one of four gates:

- **Qualified in → book the demo now.** Founder pulls up the calendar on the call, proposes 2-3 specific times, confirms the audience (buyer + champion + user, or at least buyer + champion), and books the demo. No "I'll follow up with times" — that's a 30% slip.
- **Qualified in but missing information → book the follow-up now.** Founder identifies the specific missing piece (a stakeholder who has to weigh in, an artifact the buyer promised, a metric that needs sizing) and books a specific 15-30-minute follow-up on the call. The follow-up has an agenda; it is not an open-ended "let's chat again."
- **Needs-second-call with a specific missing person or artifact.** Similar to above, but with the specific missing element named on the call and the second call framed around it: "let's get Ramesh's CFO in a 20-minute session to walk through the ROI math — I have Tuesday 3pm or Thursday 10am open."
- **Qualified out → close the loop honestly.** Founder names the disqualifier — "GitLab, not GitHub; we don't have first-class GitLab support today" — and either refers the buyer to an alternative (partner or competitor if appropriate) or offers to reach back out when a specific unlock condition trips. The buyer is not left on a hopeful nurture.

The universal rule: **the deal moves stage or closes lost on the call, not in the founder's inbox two days later.** Chapter 5 develops the next-step discipline as its own concept; discovery is where the discipline is first enforced.

### Discovery calls are also mod-101 discovery interviews

For the founder, every discovery call is dual-purpose. It is a sales call — she is qualifying the deal, sizing the pain, positioning the product. It is also a *customer development interview* — she is learning how buyers in this segment describe the pain, which parts of her hypothesis are wrong, which parts of the product are surprisingly load-bearing, which parts are surprisingly ignored. This is the customer-development-fusion property from Chapter 1.

The practical consequence: after every discovery call, the founder should spend 5-10 minutes writing three notes:

1. **The new language the buyer used** — words for the pain that differ from the founder's current language. These update the value hypothesis (Chapter 2), the demo narrative (Chapter 4), and the marketing copy (mod-108).
2. **The surprise** — anything the buyer said that the founder did not expect. Surprises are the highest-signal input to iterating the ICP (mod-104) and the pricing pack (mod-106).
3. **The next-step commitment** — what was agreed on the call, with the date, so the follow-up email (Chapter 5) can be sent within 2 hours of hanging up.

The dual-purpose nature is what makes founder-led discovery worth doing directly — the AE, later, will do only the sales half. The founder captures the development half now, while the motion is still being characterised.

### The consultative posture — how discovery avoids the interrogation failure mode

Discovery frameworks (SPICED, MEDDIC) are checklists. Discovery *practice* is a conversation. The two have to be reconciled through posture.

- **Ask open, listen long.** Every question is open-ended ("walk me through how your team handles X today"), then the founder shuts up and lets the buyer talk for 60-120 seconds without interrupting. The founder's most-common mistake is filling silence.
- **Paraphrase before advancing.** "So — if I've got it right, you're dealing with X, which is showing up as Y in your KPI, and the pattern started when Z shipped in Q3?" Paraphrase demonstrates listening, confirms accuracy, and gives the buyer a chance to correct or expand.
- **Diagnose before prescribing.** The founder does not offer the product as a solution until the pain is characterised, sized, and acknowledged. Prescribing early ("we do exactly that!") converts the call from consultative to transactional and shuts down the buyer's willingness to share pain.
- **Ask about the past, not the future.** Mom Test (mod-101) rules still apply — "when did that last happen?" is a stronger question than "would you use a tool that fixed this?" Past behaviour predicts future purchase; hypothetical willingness does not.
- **Comfort with silence and comfort with "no."** The founder who cannot sit with a 4-second silence, or who cannot hear "we're not interested" without immediately trying to re-open the deal, converts every call into a soft-pitch and loses the discovery signal. Silence is a feature; a clean "no" is a gift (it disqualifies fast).

The consultative posture is what makes 8-12 questions feel like a conversation. Without it, the same 8-12 questions feel like a checkpoint.

## Concrete example — a Loomly SPICED discovery call

Jane (Loomly founder) runs a 30-minute discovery call with Ramesh (VP Eng at Acme Observability), booked from the cold sequence in Chapter 2's example. Sasha (Engineering Manager, the champion candidate) is on the call.

**Opening (2 min):** Jane frames — "thanks for the time; I'd like to spend most of this call understanding your situation and pain, then figure out together whether we're a fit; if yes, book a proper demo with the folks who'd use it."

**Situation (4 min):** Ramesh confirms 65 engineers, distributed team, GitHub Cloud primary, deploys ~2x/day. Sasha confirms she runs a 12-engineer platform-tooling team.

**Pain (7 min):** Jane asks Sasha to walk her through the last week's PR review workflow. Sasha describes a specific case: a critical bug fix PR sat for 4 days waiting for review, blocked a customer's SLA, and required Sasha to Slack-chase three engineers individually. Jane paraphrases; Sasha confirms and adds two more examples from the last month.

**Impact (5 min):** Jane works the math with Ramesh — cycle time drifted from 8 hours (Q2) to 3 days (Q3), delayed the November release by two weeks, contributed to a customer's contract slipping into Q1 (~$180K delayed revenue). Ramesh nods; he had these numbers but had not connected them to the cycle-time cause. The number is now shared.

**Critical event (3 min):** Ramesh volunteers — Q4 board meeting in 5 weeks, cycle-time reduction is one of two stated goals, board expects to see a mechanism. This is the deal's natural close date.

**Decision (4 min):** Ramesh confirms — he owns the tools budget up to $25K, no procurement gate below that, Sasha will be the primary champion, VP Eng peer group will influence but not gate. Jane asks specifically "who else at Acme would want to weigh in on adopting a new dev-productivity tool?" — Ramesh mentions the CTO would want to see it once, not as gate. That's the "any blockers?" question surfacing.

**Verdict + next step (5 min):** Jane summarises — strong ICP fit (Layer-1 and Layer-2 from mod-104 all pass), pain sized at ~$180K/year against a $12K ACV (15x ROI story), critical event locked, decision path clean. Jane: "I'd like to run a 45-minute demo with you, Sasha, and one senior engineer from Sasha's team so you can see the shape and Sasha can drive the workflow. Tuesday 2pm or Thursday 11am — which works?" Ramesh: Thursday 11am. Booked, calendar invites sent from Jane's phone before the call ends.

Total call: 30 minutes. Verdict: qualified in. Next step: booked on call. Post-call, Jane writes her three notes (new language: "Slack-chasing three engineers individually" — this is the phrase she'll re-use in the demo narrative; surprise: Ramesh had the numbers but had not connected them causally to cycle time — that's a discovery-vs-narrative insight; next-step commitment: Thursday 11am demo, with Sasha + one senior engineer).

The call could have gone the other way. Had Ramesh said "we're on GitLab," Jane would have named the disqualifier, offered to loop back in Q2 when GitLab support was on the roadmap, and closed the loop clean. That is a qualified-out with a reason; it is a healthy outcome and a legible data point for the win-loss pattern (Chapter 7).

## Common failure patterns

- **The skip-discovery-jump-to-demo trap.** Prospect says "sure, can you demo?" on the first email reply; founder schedules a demo without discovery. Demo becomes a feature tour, no pain sized, deal never closes. Fix: every first meeting is a discovery call, not a demo, no matter how much the buyer says "just show me the product."
- **The unsized-pain trap.** Discovery covers Situation and Pain but stops there. Impact ("what does this cost you?") is uncomfortable to ask so the founder skips it. Deal opens without a fundable pain number and stalls at proposal. Fix: work the pain-sizing arithmetic with the buyer on the call; it is not optional.
- **The no-critical-event trap.** Discovery surfaces pain and impact but no critical event. Deal has no natural close date and drifts. Six months later it's still "open." Fix: ask specifically "what's the trigger that forces a decision by a date?" — if there isn't one, either surface one ("planning cycle? board? renewal?") or downgrade the deal's stage confidence.
- **The role-not-name trap.** Founder exits discovery with "their VP Eng" and "someone in procurement." Deal stalls because names were never surfaced. Fix: exit with named individuals for champion, buyer, and (per ACV) economic buyer; ask "who specifically will need to be in the room?"
- **The interrogation trap.** Founder reads 47 questions from a spreadsheet checklist. Buyer disengages. Fix: 8-12 questions, held as coverage list not linear script, asked conversationally in whatever order the conversation flows.
- **The soft-pitch-instead-of-discovery trap.** Founder pitches the product for 25 of the 30 minutes; buyer answers 3 questions total. Founder writes the call up as "great discovery" when in fact no discovery happened. Fix: the founder talks < 30% of the call; the buyer talks > 70%.
- **The wrong-framework-for-ACV trap.** Founder uses SPICED on a $300K enterprise deal without Economic-buyer identification or Decision-process mapping. Deal slips a quarter at procurement no one saw coming. Fix: upgrade to MEDDIC / MEDDPICC when ACV and complexity demand it.
- **The MEDDIC-on-a-Stripe-transaction trap.** Founder uses MEDDIC's full 8-letter discipline on a $6K/year self-serve deal. The buyer feels over-managed, the deal slows for no gain. Fix: match framework to ACV; SPICED is enough for transactional cycles.
- **The no-verdict trap.** Founder ends the call with "sounds great, I'll send some info over." No verdict, no next step, deal enters pipeline debt. Fix: state the verdict explicitly on the call and either book the next step live or close-lose with a reason.
- **The ignoring-blockers trap.** Discovery surfaces champion + buyer, doesn't ask about anyone who might say no. At proposal, security lead vetoes on a compliance requirement no one raised. Fix: ask "who else needs to weigh in?" and "any concerns from security / IT / legal / procurement?" during Decision.
- **The consultant-not-vendor trap.** Founder over-consults, gives away hours of diagnosis without asking for the deal. Buyer thanks the founder for the insight and goes to build it in-house or hire a competitor. Fix: consultative posture is the *method*, not the goal — the ask for the demo (or the next step) still has to close the call.
- **The no-post-call-notes trap.** Founder does not write up the three notes (new language / surprise / next-step commitment) within 2 hours of the call. Notes drift; the customer-development half of the call is lost. Fix: 5-10 minutes of notes immediately, before the next meeting.

## Summary

- Discovery is the founder-led conversation whose objective is to **find the pain, size the pain, identify the buyer, and gate the deal in or out with a calendared next step**. It is bidirectional, consultative, and verdict-producing.
- **SPICED** (Situation, Pain, Impact, Critical event, Decision) — the Winning by Design framework — is the module's default for ACV < $50K and mid-market cycles. Flexible arc, 8-12 questions, held as coverage list.
- **MEDDIC** (Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion) upgrades SPICED for larger ACV and formal buying committees. **MEDDPICC** adds Paper process and Competition for enterprise deals.
- The framework depends on ACV band: SPICED under $50K, MEDDIC $50K-$250K, MEDDPICC above $250K. Founder consciously upgrades as the motion moves up-market; the upgrade is not automatic.
- **Sizing pain** — converting a mild pain to a fundable pain in dollars, hours, or missed goals — is where most founders under-invest. The arithmetic is worked *with* the buyer on the call, producing a shared number the champion carries internally.
- **Buyer identification** exits discovery with *names* — not roles — for champion, buyer, and (per ACV) economic buyer. "Any blockers?" surfaces the fifth-role hidden veto.
- The **written 8-12-question script** is what makes the motion transferrable (Chapter 8). The founder holds it as a safety net for coverage, not as a linear checkpoint.
- The **consultative posture** — open questions, long listening, paraphrase before prescribing, past behaviour not hypothetical, comfort with silence and clean no's — is what makes 8-12 questions feel like a conversation instead of an interrogation.
- The **next-step gate** exits every call through one of four verdicts: qualified in + demo booked on call, qualified in + follow-up booked on call, needs-second-call + specific missing element, or qualified out + reason coded. No "let me follow up later."
- Every founder-led discovery call is **dual-purpose** — a sales call *and* a customer-development interview. Post-call, the founder writes three notes: new language, surprise, next-step commitment. This is what makes founding sales worth doing directly instead of delegating.
- Chapter 4 picks up at the **demo** — the stage discovery gates into, run against the pain sized in discovery.
