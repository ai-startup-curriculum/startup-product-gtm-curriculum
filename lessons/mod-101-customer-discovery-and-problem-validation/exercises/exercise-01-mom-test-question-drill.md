# Exercise 01 — Mom Test Question Drill

**Estimated time:** 2 hours
**Chapter link:** [`02-mom-test-discipline.md`](../02-mom-test-discipline.md)
**Prerequisite reading:** [Rob Fitzpatrick — *The Mom Test*](https://www.momtestbook.com/) (skim chapters 1–3)

## Problem statement

A founder's most damaging habit is asking interviewees to grade their idea instead of asking them about their life. This exercise trains your reflex to spot and rewrite the pattern before it enters an interview script.

You will be given a set of bad questions — the kind that dominate the first interview scripts most founders write — and your task is to rewrite them into questions that follow the three Mom Test rules from Chapter 2:

1. Talk about their life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.

## Requirements

Complete all three parts. Deliver as a single markdown file under your working folder (name it `exercise-01-mom-test.md`).

### Part A — Diagnose (20 min)

For each of the ten questions below, in a one-line rationale, name **which** of the three Mom Test rules it violates, and identify the specific founder-fishing pattern (asking for a future opinion, pitching disguised as a question, asking a hypothetical willingness-to-pay, etc.).

1. "Would you use a tool that automates weekly team retros?"
2. "Don't you think it's crazy how much time engineers spend in code review?"
3. "How much would you pay for an AI assistant that writes your PR descriptions?"
4. "If we built a Slack bot that notified you when your PR was stale, would you install it?"
5. "Do you think other engineering managers would find this useful?"
6. "What do you think about our idea to make onboarding faster?"
7. "Wouldn't it be great if you never had to write another status update?"
8. "Would you rather have Slack or email notifications for stalled PRs?"
9. "Would you pay $200/month for something like this?"
10. "Would you tell your friends about our product?"

### Part B — Rewrite (60 min)

Rewrite each of the ten questions from Part A into a Mom-Test-compliant version. Constraints on the rewrites:

- Every rewrite must be **past-tense** and about a **specific instance** in the interviewee's life.
- No use of the words **would, could, might, imagine, if, hypothetically, in the future**.
- No reference to your product, your idea, or your team's plans.
- Where a follow-up "why?" is the point of the question, write both the question and the follow-up.

For each rewrite, add a one-line note describing what information you now expect to get that you did not get from the original.

### Part C — Author your own five (40 min)

Write **five original** past-tense, life-focused questions for a discovery interview about a topic of your choice — either your own startup idea, a hypothetical one, or one drawn from a public example (Superhuman, Airbnb, Loom, Notion, etc.). Constraints:

- Anchor the questions to a specific **ICP** (name it at the top of the section — who this interview would be with).
- Include at least one **switch-story** question (Chapter 4 forces framing) — one question that would surface the moment they last moved from one solution to another and why.
- Include a **commitment-ladder** ask at the end (a real ask you would make at the close of the interview — referral, prototype session, LOI, deposit).

## Starter guidance

- Read Chapter 2 (`02-mom-test-discipline.md`) end-to-end before you start. The rewrite table in that chapter is your model.
- For each bad question, ask yourself: "what am I actually trying to learn?" Then write a past-tense specific question that would reveal it directly. If you cannot answer "what am I trying to learn?", the question is a pitch and should be deleted, not rewritten.
- The correct rewrite is rarely a single question — it is often a *pair*: a specific past-tense question, then a "why?" or "walk me through what you did next" follow-up. Both belong on the page.
- Do not soften bad questions ("would you find this useful?" → "would you probably find this useful?"). Rewriting means replacing the shape of the question, not the tone.
- Watch for the trap of "would you..." questions disguised as past-tense ones. "Have you ever felt like X?" is still asking for an opinion; "the last time X happened, what did you do?" is a past-tense question.

## Acceptance criteria

Your submission is complete when:

- [ ] Part A has all 10 diagnoses, each naming the specific Mom Test rule violated **and** the founder-fishing pattern.
- [ ] Part B has all 10 rewrites; none of them uses **would / could / might / imagine / if / hypothetically**; every one is past-tense and specific; every one has the expected-information note.
- [ ] Part C has 5 original questions with a named ICP at the top; at least one is a switch-story question; the section ends with a concrete commitment-ladder ask; nothing pitches the interviewer's own product.
- [ ] The whole document could be handed to a co-founder and used as the raw material for a real interview script tomorrow, without further editing.

## Common ways this exercise goes wrong

- **Rewrites that just soften the bad question.** "How much would you pay?" → "What do you think a fair price would be?" is still hypothetical. The right rewrite is "What are you currently paying for tools that touch this workflow?"
- **Rewrites that pitch.** "When was the last time you *needed* our kind of tool?" still centres the interviewer's product. Kill the reference.
- **Part C questions that are not about a specific ICP.** "Tell me about a time you were frustrated" is too broad. Anchor to a workflow and a role.
- **Skipping the switch-story question in Part C.** The forces framing (Chapter 4) is what turns Part C from a competent script into an information-producing one. Do not skip it.
