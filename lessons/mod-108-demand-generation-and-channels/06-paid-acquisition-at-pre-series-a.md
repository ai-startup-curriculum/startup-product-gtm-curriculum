# Paid Acquisition at Pre-Series-A — Guardrails, Attribution, and the Growth-Curve Trap

## Motivation

Paid acquisition is the fastest channel in this module to *measure* and the most dangerous channel in this module to *scale before PMF*. A founder can spin up a Google Ads account this afternoon, wire a conversion pixel, load a $10K credit-card daily limit, and by tomorrow morning have hundreds of clicks and a first CPA number. The dashboard looks like progress; the pipeline dashboard, six months later, tells a very different story — the acquired customers churn twice as fast as organically-acquired ones, the CAC drifts up as spend scales, the auction inflates because two better-funded competitors joined the same keyword set, and the "growth curve" the paid programme produced turns out to be a growth curve nobody could retain.

The founder-facing failure is deceptively simple: **paid channels let you buy leads. They do not let you buy a business.** A paid channel producing 200 sign-ups a month against a product that retains 40% of them at day 90 is a paid channel spending twice as much per retained customer as the CAC dashboard reports. A paid channel producing 200 sign-ups a month against a product with no working retention loop is a paid channel that will bankrupt the company on schedule the moment the round closes and spend accelerates. This is the *growth-curve trap* — buying a top-of-funnel that the product cannot yet sustain, then treating the resulting number as evidence the product-market-fit has arrived.

The failure mode this chapter exists to catch: **the founder — usually under pressure from a board deck deadline, a fundraise narrative, or a competitor's visible growth — turns on paid at $10-30K/month before the retention curve, the pricing pack, or the ICP is nailed down; the numbers look plausible for two quarters because early cohort retention hasn't reported yet; the founder scales to $80-150K/month against a Series A raise; six months post-A the retention curve arrives, CAC : LTV inverts, and the runway that was supposed to last 24 months lasts 11.**

The failure mode this chapter *also* exists to catch is the opposite one, less spectacular but nearly as costly: **the founder rejects paid on principle — "we're a bottoms-up dev-tools company, we don't do ads" — and never runs a small, disciplined paid test on any channel. Two years later a competitor has captured 40% of the branded-search real estate, a substantial fraction of the ICP now finds the competitor first, and the founder cannot recover the position without a much larger budget.** Paid is not always right; but rejecting it without a test is as diagnostic-invisible as running it without guardrails.

This chapter builds the paid motion the way it actually works at pre-Series-A: **search / social / retargeting as the three surface families**, **guardrails on spend**, **attribution honesty** (a specific version of Chapter 2's Gate 4 applied to paid), **the pre-PMF trap** (buying a growth curve you cannot retain), and **the small-experiment discipline** that lets a founder learn whether paid works for the ICP without committing runway to a scale-stage programme.

## Core concepts

### The tradition — direct-response, performance marketing, and the modern amendments

The intellectual tradition of paid acquisition goes back further than any other channel this module covers. The direct-response canon — Claude Hopkins's *Scientific Advertising* (1923), David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) — established the principles that still apply: **every ad is measured against a defined action; every campaign is tested against a control; the cost per action is what matters, not the cost per impression** ([Hopkins — *Scientific Advertising*, public-domain edition](https://scientificadvertising.com/)). Modern performance marketing (Google Ads from 2000, Facebook Ads from 2007, LinkedIn Ads from 2011) inherited the direct-response discipline and industrialised it — auction-based pricing, real-time bidding, conversion pixels, retargeting, look-alike audiences.

For a B2B seed-stage founder, three modern amendments to the classical direct-response tradition matter:

**Amendment 1 — the platform-attribution shift (Apple's ATT, iOS 14+, 2021-ongoing; Chrome's third-party cookie deprecation, ongoing).** The tracking infrastructure that made paid attribution defensible in the 2010s (deterministic cross-device pixels, third-party cookies, mobile-app IDFAs) has substantially degraded. Meta's post-iOS-14 conversion signal is 20-40% weaker than pre-2021, and every platform now models a growing fraction of "conversions" rather than observing them directly ([Meta Blog — "About Apple's iOS 14.5 update"](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/331612538028890)). This does not make paid unmeasurable; it makes attribution honesty (Chapter 2 Gate 4) more, not less, load-bearing.

**Amendment 2 — LLM-answered-search substitution on informational queries (2023-ongoing).** A growing fraction of the informational queries that paid search used to serve are now answered by an LLM before the user reaches a search-engine results page. The impact on paid search is not uniform — **branded queries and transactional queries are largely unaffected; informational queries are heavily impacted.** Paid budgets that were productive on the informational tier in 2019-2022 are less productive in 2025-2026; budgets concentrated on branded-defence and transactional-intent queries are largely unaffected (see mod-108 Chapter 3 on the LLM-cited-search shift).

**Amendment 3 — the AI-generated ad-creative shift.** Ad creative is now cheap to produce (image, video, and copy variants generated at near-zero marginal cost). The consequence is not that creative is unimportant; it is that *distinctive* creative is more important — the auction now shows every advertiser plausible variants of the same generated image, and the ads that break out are the ones with authentic first-party imagery, real founder voice, and specific claims the competition cannot copy.

The three amendments sharpen the operational shape of paid — they do not overturn its direct-response fundamentals. The founder's job is still to instrument a defined action, measure cost per action, and iterate.

### The three surface families — search, social, retargeting

Paid at pre-Series-A has three surface families with distinct operating shapes. Each has its own intent signal, its own auction dynamics, and its own failure modes. A founder does not need to be running all three at any given time — most seed-stage paid programmes concentrate on one, sometimes two.

**Family 1 — search.** Google Ads (primary), Bing Ads (secondary), Reddit search ads (niche). The user's query signals intent: someone searching "renovate config alternatives" or "detect stale services monorepo" has a specific problem she is currently trying to solve, and the ad is answering the query. Search is the highest-intent surface available in paid and, when the query matches the product, has the highest conversion rate of any paid channel. Sub-types by intent tier:

- **Branded-defence.** Bidding on the company's own brand name to defend the top of the SERP against competitors. Cheap, high-intent, near-100% ICP match. **Almost always the right first paid dollar at seed** — it protects the brand-search close (Chapter 3, inbound attribution) from a competitor who has bid your brand.
- **Competitor-brand.** Bidding on competitor brand names (where the ad platform allows). High-intent, medium-quality traffic (people looking for the competitor may not always convert to the challenger). Legal in the US and most jurisdictions but strategically variable — competitor-bid can invite counter-bidding wars.
- **Transactional-intent.** Bidding on queries that indicate active evaluation ("best X for Y", "X vs Y", "X pricing", "X alternatives"). Medium-to-high intent, moderate volume, sometimes highly competitive at high-value ACV bands.
- **Informational-intent.** Bidding on queries that indicate problem-recognition but not necessarily active evaluation ("how to X", "why my Y is failing"). Lower intent, higher volume, most affected by LLM substitution. Often better served by inbound content (Chapter 3) than by paid search at seed stage.

**Family 2 — social.** LinkedIn Ads (dominant for B2B mid-market and enterprise), Meta / Instagram (SMB and consumer B2B), X / Twitter Ads (niche technical audiences), YouTube Ads (video-native audiences), Reddit Ads (niche communities), TikTok Ads (consumer-adjacent B2B). Intent is *inferred from demographic + behavioural targeting*, not stated by the user — the auction serves ads to users whose profile matches a targeting definition, not to users who typed a query. Consequence: **the ad is interrupting the user's attention rather than answering it**; conversion rates are lower, but the top-of-funnel volume can be substantially higher. Sub-types by targeting:

- **Job-title + firmographic targeting** (LinkedIn's core capability). Serves ads to users whose LinkedIn profile matches specified job titles + company attributes. High-precision for B2B but expensive per click (LinkedIn CPCs are typically 3-10× Google Ads).
- **Look-alike audiences.** The platform builds an audience "similar to" a seed list of existing customers. Precision drops as the look-alike expands; cost per click moderate; often useful for scaling a targeting approach that is working at the direct level.
- **Interest / behaviour targeting.** Users tagged by platform-observed behaviour (page follows, group memberships, engagement patterns). Broadest and least precise; useful for top-of-funnel awareness at scale, poor for conversion-focused campaigns at seed.

**Family 3 — retargeting.** Serving ads to users who have already visited the site, downloaded a resource, or engaged with content. Highest conversion rate of the three families because the audience is warm; smallest audience size because it is bounded by prior site traffic. Retargeting is a *complement* to another primary channel (inbound content, outbound outreach, search) rather than a standalone acquisition motion — it re-engages users the other channels already reached.

Retargeting has been degraded by the platform-attribution shift (third-party cookie deprecation reduced cross-site retargeting audience size by 30-60%). First-party retargeting (people who signed up but did not activate; people who visited pricing but did not sign up) still works well; third-party retargeting (people who visited any similar site) works less well.

### Guardrails on spend — the pre-Series-A budget shape

Paid at pre-Series-A is not a scaling motion. It is a **learning motion**, funded to the level where a founder can answer a specific question ("does search paid work for our ICP at a defensible CAC?" or "does LinkedIn targeting on our exact ICP produce ICP-fit leads?") without committing runway to a scale-stage programme. Three guardrails structure the budget shape.

**Guardrail 1 — the total-budget ceiling.** At pre-Series-A, paid budget should not exceed **10-25% of gross-margin-adjusted new revenue** on a rolling 6-month basis, and should have an absolute hard ceiling in the **$5-25K/month range** during the learning phase. A team with $50K/month of new ARR at 78% gross margin should have a paid ceiling in the $4-10K/month range; a team with $10K/month of new ARR should not be running paid at $10K/month full stop. The ceiling exists because paid does not create value by itself — it *converts* value the underlying product economics support, and spending past what the product economics support burns cash on customers who cannot pay it back.

**Guardrail 2 — the per-channel test-budget floor.** A paid experiment on a specific channel needs enough spend to produce a statistically defensible signal — usually **at least $3-5K of spend and 50+ conversion events** over a defined test window (typically 4-8 weeks). Below the floor, the CAC number is noise, and the "does this work?" question cannot be answered. Running $500/month across five channels produces zero defensible answers; running $3K/month on one channel for two months produces one.

**Guardrail 3 — the payback-period stopcock.** A paid channel that has run past its time-to-signal window (Chapter 2 Gate 3: 3-6 months of consistent spend + ≥50 closed deals) and cannot demonstrate a **payback period ≤ 18 months** on the acquired cohort should be paused, not scaled. Paying back paid CAC in 24+ months at pre-Series-A cash levels is often mathematically fatal — the runway is shorter than the payback cycle, and the paid spend consumes the very cash the payback would eventually return.

The three guardrails compound. A founder facing a decision to double paid spend can ask three questions in order: **is the total budget still under 25% of gross-margin-adjusted new revenue?** **is the per-channel test still funded above the $3-5K floor for the test window?** **has payback demonstrated ≤ 18 months on the last cohort of acquired customers?** Any "no" is a hold-and-diagnose, not a scale-up.

### Attribution honesty — the paid-specific extensions

Chapter 2 Gate 4 covered attribution as a general discipline. Paid has three additional attribution risks that a general framework misses.

**Risk 1 — platform-reported attribution is aspirational.** Meta's Ads Manager, Google Ads, and LinkedIn Campaign Manager report "conversions" as a mix of *observed* conversions (a pixel fired) and *modelled* conversions (the platform's algorithm estimated based on incomplete data). The modelled fraction is now 20-50% on most B2B campaigns and can be much higher after iOS 14.5. **A platform-reported CAC of $220 may reflect a true CAC of $350 or more.** The correct baseline is the CAC computed from the CRM against the paid-attributed sub-cohort — not the number the platform's dashboard shows.

**Risk 2 — last-touch attribution over-credits paid on multi-touch buyer journeys.** A prospect discovers the product via a Hacker News post (Chapter 3, inbound); visits the site, does not sign up; sees a retargeting ad two days later; clicks the ad; signs up. Last-touch attribution credits the retargeting ad for the acquisition; the actual work was done by the inbound post. If the founder scales retargeting spend based on last-touch attribution, she is scaling the wrong channel. **Report first-touch and last-touch side-by-side; on multi-touch journeys, use position-weighted or U-shaped attribution as the primary measure and last-touch as a secondary signal.**

**Risk 3 — the branded-search leakage problem.** A prospect discovers the product via any non-paid channel (inbound, outbound, community, referral); a week later, she Googles the company name to find the site again; the branded-search ad appears; she clicks the ad; she signs up. Paid gets last-touch credit for a customer paid did not acquire — the ad simply appeared at the moment the user was already committed. **Branded-search ads inflate paid's apparent CAC-efficiency; they are still worth running (branded-defence) but their conversions should be tagged and reported separately from acquisition paid.** The rule of thumb: **branded-search CAC is not comparable to non-branded paid CAC**; blending them is a common form of attribution self-deception.

**The paid-attribution audit.** Once per quarter, run the audit on paid attribution: pick 10 closed-won deals attributed to a paid channel; for each, reconstruct the full first-touch-to-close journey from CRM history + self-reported source + email history + paid platform reports. Count: how many were *actually* first discovered through the paid channel (not just last-touched by it)? If the answer is less than 6 of 10, the paid channel is over-credited on last-touch attribution and the effective CAC is materially higher than the dashboard reports.

### The pre-PMF trap — why paid before PMF is dangerous

The most common way paid destroys a company at pre-Series-A: **the founder turns on paid spend before the product has demonstrated retention.** The mechanism, in three steps:

**Step 1 — paid produces top-of-funnel volume the product cannot yet sustain.** Sign-ups appear on the dashboard. Free-trial starts, credit-card conversions, MQL volumes climb. The metric that the paid channel is designed to move (cost per sign-up, cost per MQL, cost per trial-start) improves as the campaign iterates. Everything looks correct.

**Step 2 — the retention curve fires 60-180 days later and the acquired cohort churns.** Because the product does not yet have PMF (the retention curve has not been characterised by mod-102, the "very disappointed" cohort is too small to define the beachhead by mod-104), the acquired customers are ICP-adjacent rather than ICP-fit — they signed up because the ad was compelling, not because the pain was acute for them. They activate at lower rates; they retain at lower rates; and by the 90-180 day mark, a substantial fraction of the cohort has churned.

**Step 3 — the CAC : LTV math inverts, but the CAC dashboard did not report it.** The founder has been running the paid campaign against an assumed LTV computed from a mixed retention baseline (organic + paid + founder-led-sales, blended). The paid-cohort retention was well below the blend, but the blended LTV number masked the difference. Six-to-twelve months later, when the paid cohort has been in the customer base long enough to compute a cohort-specific retention curve, the paid-cohort LTV is 40-60% below the blend, and the paid CAC : LTV ratio is 1 : 1.2 rather than the 1 : 3 the founder thought she had.

The recovery from Step 3 is painful and slow. Paid spend has to be cut; the cohort's retention deficit cannot be recovered; the runway consumed is not recoverable; the fundraise narrative that included the paid growth curve now has to be rewritten. Andrew Chen's "law of shitty click-throughs" (Chapter 7) will accelerate the failure — the channel would have decayed anyway, but the pre-PMF cohort churn made the decay indistinguishable from acquisition failure.

**The rule of thumb, from the practitioner consensus (Balfour, Ellis, Skok, Chen):** do not scale paid past the small-experiment budget until **(a) the mod-102 PMF signal has fired at ~40% "very disappointed" on the target ICP**, and **(b) the organically-acquired cohort has demonstrated retention parity or better against the non-paid benchmarks**, and **(c) the paid-cohort's 90-day retention (from a small paid test) is within 10 percentage points of the organic-cohort's 90-day retention**. All three conditions in place before spend scales past the learning-motion ceiling. If any of the three is missing, the paid programme stays at learning-motion scale — funded, disciplined, instrumented, but not scaled — until the missing condition is met.

**Two exceptions where paid pre-PMF can be defensible.**

- **Branded-defence.** Bidding on the company's own brand name is not a pre-PMF risk — it protects the brand-search close from competitor bids and does not create the growth-curve-trap dynamics.
- **A tightly-scoped ICP-validation test.** A small, time-boxed paid experiment (≤ $5K over 4-6 weeks) designed to answer whether a specific ICP segment can be reached through a specific paid surface at any defensible CAC. The test is a learning exercise, not a scaling exercise; the output is a yes/no on the segment/surface pair, not a growth curve.

### The small-experiment discipline

For a pre-Series-A founder who wants to answer the "does paid work for our ICP?" question, the small-experiment discipline replaces the "turn it on and scale it" instinct. The discipline has four elements.

**Element 1 — one experiment, one channel, one hypothesis at a time.** Not five channels at $1K/month each; one channel at $3-5K/month against one hypothesis. The hypothesis is written down before the test starts and reads like: *"Google Ads on high-intent transactional queries in the [X] category will produce ICP-fit sign-ups at a CPA below $Y over 6 weeks."* If Y is not defined before the test starts, the test has no exit criterion and the founder will not know whether to conclude the experiment succeeded or failed.

**Element 2 — the test window is 4-8 weeks with defined stop criteria.** Not "we'll see how it goes"; a defined start date, a defined end date, defined stop criteria (a CPA that stays above the budget for two consecutive weeks; a conversion volume below 20/week for three consecutive weeks; the total-budget-ceiling being reached). The test either succeeds (per hypothesis), fails (stop criteria triggered), or produces an inconclusive result (window closes without either — in which case the correct decision is to extend or to run a second test with different parameters, not to keep spending indefinitely).

**Element 3 — the four-gate diagnosis (Chapter 2) is run on the test cohort at 6 weeks and 12 weeks.** Gate 1 (ICP-fit) at 6 weeks: are the sign-ups from the paid channel Layer-1 ICP-fit at the same rate as founder-led-sales sign-ups? Gate 2 (unit economics) at 12 weeks: does the fully-loaded CAC (including the founder's time spent managing the campaign, not just ad spend) fit inside the payback envelope? Gate 3 (time-to-signal) at 12 weeks: has enough spend accrued and enough conversions closed to compute a defensible CAC (typically ≥ 50 conversions and ≥ $10K spend)? Gate 4 (attribution) run throughout: self-reported source data, platform-vs-CRM cross-check, branded-search separation.

**Element 4 — the write-up.** Every experiment produces a written note at the end of the window: what was the hypothesis, what was the actual result, what was the four-gate diagnosis, what is the recommendation (scale, iterate, kill, defer), and what is the next hypothesis (if any) the experiment surfaced. The write-up is the artifact that turns paid from a set of ad accounts into a *learning programme*. Without the write-up, the founder repeats the same tests with the same assumptions and burns budget without accumulating knowledge.

### Choosing the surface — a channel-market-fit-first sequence

The channel-choice for paid follows the same three-question test Chapter 1 uses for the primary-channel decision, but with paid-specific weighting. In order:

**Question 1 — where does the ICP behave in a way paid can reach?** LinkedIn Ads work when the ICP is on LinkedIn and can be targeted by job-title + firmographic filters — usually **VP-level buyers at mid-market and enterprise companies**. Google Ads work when the ICP searches for solutions using recognisable transactional queries — usually **problem-aware buyers across most B2B ICPs**. Meta / Instagram work when the ICP is on those platforms and can be reached at rates the ACV supports — usually **SMB buyers and consumer-adjacent B2B**, rarely at effective rates for enterprise. Reddit works when the ICP concentrates in specific subreddits — usually **technical audiences on niche subreddits**. Match the surface to where the ICP actually is; do not choose Meta because the founder personally uses it if the ICP doesn't.

**Question 2 — what does the CAC math support?** ACV band constraints (Chapter 2 Gate 2). At $99-499/month self-serve, the paid CAC ceiling is around $200-800; only high-quality search and specific look-alike social work inside that envelope. At $10-30K/year mid-market SaaS, the paid CAC ceiling is $3-9K, which supports LinkedIn Ads and Google Ads on transactional queries. At $100K+/year enterprise, the paid CAC ceiling is $25K+ per closed deal, which supports account-based advertising and targeted display alongside search — though enterprise is often better served by partnerships and events than by paid (Chapter 1, sub-motion catalogue).

**Question 3 — what does the funnel supply chain need?** Paid produces top-of-funnel volume that has to be converted through the same downstream funnel the other channels feed. If the funnel is broken at the demo-conversion stage (the mod-105 discovery script is producing low advance rates), scaling paid will produce more meetings the funnel cannot convert. If the pricing pack (mod-106) does not support the sign-up experience the ad is promising, paid CAC will inflate on refund-and-abandon dynamics. **Paid is downstream of the funnel; fix the funnel first, then supply it.**

The candidate that scores highest against the three questions is the first paid experiment; the second-scoring candidate is the second experiment (run sequentially, not in parallel, at pre-Series-A scale). Everything else defers.

## Concrete example — Loomly's Q2 branded-defence + LinkedIn ICP-test

Loomly's Q1 portfolio (Chapters 1, 2, 4): primary = founder-led outbound; experimental = inbound content + SEO; paid = explicitly on the "not now" list, deferred to Q2 for a bounded test. Jane's Q2 decision: run two small paid experiments — a persistent branded-defence campaign, and a bounded ICP-validation test on LinkedIn Ads.

**Experiment 1 — branded-defence on Google Ads (persistent).**

- **Hypothesis.** A competitor (Renovate.io) has begun bidding on "loomly" branded queries and is capturing an estimated 15-20% of the branded-search clicks that would otherwise land on Loomly's site. Bidding on the branded terms defends the top of the SERP at low cost.
- **Setup.** Campaign: `[Brand] Loomly`. Keywords: `loomly`, `loomly.io`, `loomly pricing`, `loomly reviews`, `loomly vs`, `loomly alternatives` (defensive), 12 keywords total. Ad copy: single ad group, single headline template referencing Loomly's core positioning statement (from mod-103). Bidding: manual CPC with a $2 max CPC; daily budget $30, monthly $900. Conversion event: sign-up form submission with `utm_source=google` + `utm_campaign=brand_defence` tag.
- **Attribution treatment.** Branded-defence conversions are tagged and *excluded* from the acquisition-CAC calculation. They are reported in a separate line item ("branded-defence conversions saved") so that they do not inflate paid's apparent acquisition efficiency.
- **6-week result.** $920 spend, 240 clicks, 38 sign-ups (16% click-to-signup), 4 closed deals. CPC $3.83 (above the $2 max because a bidding-war episode with Renovate.io in weeks 3-4 pushed CPCs to $5-8 for two weeks). Branded-search CPC settled back to $2-3 by week 5 as Renovate.io reduced its bidding.
- **Recommendation.** Continue branded-defence at $900/month indefinitely. This is not an acquisition motion; it is a brand-protection cost that runs at less than 1% of monthly new revenue and defends 15-20 sign-ups/month that would otherwise be lost to the competitor's cross-brand bid.

**Experiment 2 — LinkedIn Ads ICP-validation test (bounded).**

- **Hypothesis.** LinkedIn Ads targeting VP-Engineering + Head-of-Platform titles at 50-500-engineer B2B SaaS companies will produce ICP-fit sign-ups at a CPA below $300 over 6 weeks of testing.
- **Setup.** Campaign objective: website conversions. Targeting: job titles (VP Engineering, Head of Platform, Head of Infrastructure, Director of Engineering), company attributes (50-500 employees, industry = Computer Software or Internet), geography (US + Canada). Estimated audience: ~12,000. Ad format: sponsored content, single-image, three variants of the pillar-page CTA from Chapter 3 ("The complete guide to stale-service detection in monorepos"). Landing page: pillar page with email-gated content download. Bidding: automated conversion bidding with a $50 max CPC ceiling. Daily budget $200, weekly $1,400, 6-week total $8,400.
- **Attribution treatment.** UTM tags: `utm_source=linkedin, utm_medium=paid_social, utm_campaign=icp_validation, utm_content={variant_name}`. Landing page form has "how did you hear about us?" (self-reported source) + email + role + company. Attribution tracked in HubSpot with first-touch primary and last-touch secondary reports.
- **6-week result.** $8,200 spend, 4,600 clicks (CPC $1.78 — well inside the ceiling), 380 form submissions (8.2% click-to-form), 92 sign-ups (24% form-to-signup), 14 opportunities passed AE qualification, 2 closed deals at $18K ACV.
- **Four-gate diagnosis.** 
  - Gate 1 (ICP-fit): 71 of 92 sign-ups are Layer-1 ICP-fit (77%). **Amber** — below the ≥80% green threshold. The 21 non-ICP-fit sign-ups came from adjacent titles (Staff Engineer, Senior SRE) whose companies are inside the firmographic tier but whose roles are not the buyer/champion. Adjustment: exclude those titles from the targeting.
  - Gate 2 (unit economics): $8,200 spend + Jane's time (10 hrs/week × 6 weeks × $200/hr = $12,000) + tooling ($200) = $20,400 fully-loaded cost; 2 closed deals → **$10,200 CAC**. Above the $8-25K band for mid-market SaaS but inside the range. Payback: $18K × 78% margin = $14,040 annual gross profit → **payback 8.7 months.** **Green** on payback; amber on CAC (upper end of band).
  - Gate 3 (time-to-signal): 6 weeks is 50% of the paid channel's time-to-signal window; 2 closed deals is well below the ≥50 defensible-CAC threshold. **Red on sample size**; the Gate 2 numbers are on a thin sample and should not be over-interpreted.
  - Gate 4 (attribution): self-reported source completion 91% (green); of the 2 closed deals, both are LinkedIn-first-touch confirmed by self-report + CRM + form data. **Green.**
- **Recommendation.** *Extend the LinkedIn test for another 6 weeks* with the targeting tightened (exclude Staff Engineer + Senior SRE titles). Do not scale spend past the $200/day ceiling. Re-diagnose at 12 weeks with a full 4-gate read; if Gate 2 stays green on payback and Gate 3 sample size is at ≥15 closed deals, the LinkedIn channel graduates to a real experimental slot for Q3 (alongside inbound, which by then will be into month 6 of its curve). If Gate 2 goes red or Gate 3 stays thin, LinkedIn goes back to "not now" and the paid learning budget rotates to a different surface (Google Ads on transactional queries — the second-scoring candidate against the three-question test).
- **Write-up.** A one-page memo captures the hypothesis, the setup, the four-gate diagnosis, the recommendation, and the next hypothesis (either "extend and re-diagnose" or "pivot to Google Ads transactional"). The memo is filed in the same location as the outbound and inbound experiment notes. Two quarters from now, Jane will read the memo before deciding the Q4 paid slot.

**What Q2 paid did *not* do.**

- Did not run Meta Ads (ICP is not on Meta at rates that justify).
- Did not run Google Ads on informational-tier queries (LLM-substitution makes them low-ROI at seed; better served by inbound).
- Did not run retargeting (no sufficient site traffic yet to build a defensible retargeting audience; deferred to Q3 when inbound content and Chapter 3's distribution flywheel have accumulated a bigger first-party audience).
- Did not exceed the total-budget ceiling ($900 branded + $8,200 LinkedIn = $9,100 total against $126K quarterly new ARR = 7.2%; comfortably inside the 10-25% envelope).
- Did not build a fundraise-narrative "growth curve" on the LinkedIn test. The 2 closed deals in 6 weeks are a learning-motion result, not an acquisition-motion result, and the internal reporting treats them accordingly.

## Common failure patterns

- **Scale-paid-before-PMF trap.** Founder turns on paid at $30K/month before mod-102 PMF signal has fired or before the retention curve is characterised. Six months in, the paid-cohort retention is 30 pp below organic; CAC : LTV inverts; runway shortens. Fix: paid stays at learning-motion budget until PMF signal + retention parity conditions are both met.
- **Skip-the-branded-defence trap.** Founder refuses to bid on the company's own brand terms ("we should get that traffic for free"); competitor bids the brand and captures 15-25% of branded-search clicks. Fix: branded-defence is nearly always the right first paid dollar, separate from the acquisition CAC calculation.
- **Blended-paid-and-branded CAC trap.** Founder reports one paid-CAC number that blends branded-defence conversions (near-zero-CAC self-serve returns) with acquisition-paid conversions. Blended CAC looks $180; separated, branded is $30 and acquisition is $420. Fix: branded and acquisition are separate line items; blending hides the true acquisition CAC.
- **Platform-reported-CAC-as-truth trap.** Founder scales spend on a channel where Meta's Ads Manager reports CAC of $220; CRM-computed CAC is $410 because modelled conversions inflated the platform number. Founder is scaling against fictional efficiency. Fix: CRM-computed CAC on closed-won revenue is the truth; platform-reported CAC is a leading indicator that requires calibration.
- **Last-touch-attribution-scales-the-wrong-channel trap.** Retargeting gets last-touch credit for customers first acquired through inbound; founder scales retargeting; inbound continues doing the work while retargeting takes credit and consumes budget. Fix: first-touch + last-touch side-by-side; multi-touch attribution as the primary view for scale decisions.
- **Small-budget-across-many-channels trap.** Founder runs $500/month each on Google, LinkedIn, Meta, Reddit, and Twitter simultaneously; no channel gets to the per-channel test-floor; none produces defensible signal. Fix: one channel at a time, $3-5K/month for 4-8 weeks; sequential tests, not parallel.
- **Turn-on-and-forget trap.** Founder sets up campaigns, walks away, checks in weekly, discovers 8 weeks later that a bidding-war episode inflated CPCs to $12 for three weeks and $6K of budget produced nothing. Fix: daily check-in during the first 2 weeks of any campaign; weekly check-in thereafter; automated budget-cap and CPC-ceiling alerts.
- **AI-generated ad-creative-copy-race-to-the-bottom trap.** Founder generates 40 ad variants with an LLM at zero marginal cost; every competitor does the same; the auction fills with visually similar plausible-generic ads; distinctive first-party creative outperforms every LLM variant. Fix: authentic creative — real founder video, real customer quotes, specific claims, distinctive brand voice — is now more important, not less, in the LLM-creative era.
- **Buy-the-growth-curve trap.** Founder ramps paid ahead of a fundraise to make the growth curve look impressive; retention on the paid cohort arrives after the round closes; the growth curve turns out to be a churn curve; the runway from the round is consumed on paid cohort attrition. Fix: fundraise narrative is grounded in retention-adjusted growth, not gross top-of-funnel; do not build a fundraise narrative on paid growth that has not yet been retention-validated.
- **Kill-branded-defence-in-cost-cutting-round trap.** Founder cuts branded-defence during a runway-extension exercise ("it's just spending money to protect what we'd get for free"); competitor immediately captures 20% of branded-search traffic; recovery requires a much bigger budget in the following year. Fix: branded-defence is a permanent cost of doing business at 0.5-1.5% of new ARR; do not treat it as discretionary.
- **Retargeting-without-first-party-audience trap.** Founder sets up retargeting campaigns before the site has enough first-party traffic to build a retargeting audience of usable size; the retargeting pool is 40 users; the campaign produces one conversion in a quarter; founder concludes "retargeting doesn't work". Fix: retargeting requires first-party audience volume; defer until inbound + owned social have accumulated 5,000+ monthly unique visitors.
- **LLM-substitution-blind-spot trap.** Founder scales paid search on informational queries ("what is service ownership") without noticing that LLM-answered search has reduced click-through on those queries by 40-70%. Spend produces impressions but not clicks. Fix: concentrate paid search on branded-defence + transactional-intent queries; leave informational queries to inbound content (Chapter 3).
- **No-hypothesis-no-stop-criteria trap.** Founder turns on a campaign with no written hypothesis and no stop criteria; runs it for 5 months; produces mediocre results; cannot say whether the experiment succeeded or failed. Fix: written hypothesis + defined stop criteria before the campaign launches; four-gate diagnosis at defined checkpoints; write-up at the end of every experiment.

## Summary

- Paid is the **fastest channel to measure and the most dangerous channel to scale before PMF**. The direct-response tradition (Hopkins, Ogilvy) still applies; three modern amendments matter: **platform-attribution degradation (iOS 14+, cookie deprecation)**, **LLM substitution on informational queries**, and **AI-generated creative parity forcing distinctive first-party creative**.
- **Three surface families**: **search** (Google, Bing; sub-tiers of branded-defence / competitor-brand / transactional / informational), **social** (LinkedIn dominant for B2B; Meta / X / Reddit / YouTube niche), **retargeting** (complements another primary channel; requires first-party audience volume).
- **Three spend guardrails**: **(1) total-budget ceiling** of 10-25% of gross-margin-adjusted new revenue and absolute $5-25K/month during learning phase; **(2) per-channel test-budget floor** of $3-5K + 50 conversions over 4-8 weeks (below the floor, the answer is noise); **(3) payback-period stopcock** — paid channels with 24+ month payback pause, not scale.
- **Attribution honesty extensions specific to paid**: **(1) platform-reported CAC is aspirational** — trust CRM-computed CAC on closed revenue; **(2) last-touch attribution over-credits paid** — report first-touch + last-touch side-by-side, multi-touch as primary; **(3) branded-search leakage** — branded-defence conversions are separate from acquisition CAC.
- **The pre-PMF trap**: paid produces top-of-funnel the product cannot yet retain; churn hits the paid cohort 60-180 days later; CAC : LTV inverts; runway consumed. **Do not scale paid past learning-motion budget until (a) PMF signal fires, (b) organic retention parity is proven, (c) paid-cohort 90-day retention is within 10 pp of organic.** Exceptions: branded-defence and small ICP-validation tests.
- **The small-experiment discipline**: **one channel, one hypothesis, one written stop criteria, one four-gate diagnosis, one write-up**. Sequential tests over parallel; the write-up is what turns paid from a set of ad accounts into a learning programme.
- **Surface choice sequence**: **(1) where is the ICP reachable through paid targeting?**, **(2) what does the CAC math support at the ACV band?**, **(3) what does the downstream funnel need?** The candidate that scores highest is the first paid experiment.
- **Loomly's Q2 example**: branded-defence (persistent, $900/month) + LinkedIn Ads ICP-validation test (bounded, $8,200 over 6 weeks). Total paid spend 7.2% of quarterly new ARR — inside the guardrail. Branded-defence produces protection value; LinkedIn produces a partial-signal that extends for a second 6 weeks rather than scaling.
- **Paid is not always right; rejecting paid without a small disciplined test is as diagnostic-invisible as scaling without guardrails.** The small-experiment discipline is what produces the defensible yes-or-no answer for each surface / ICP combination.
- **Paid does not create value; it converts value the underlying product economics support.** If retention, activation, and pricing are not yet solved, paid does not fix them — it accelerates the deficit. Chapter 7 develops why *every* working paid channel eventually decays (Andrew Chen's law of shitty click-throughs) — and why the founder's job is a channel-experimentation cadence, not a hunt for a magic channel.
