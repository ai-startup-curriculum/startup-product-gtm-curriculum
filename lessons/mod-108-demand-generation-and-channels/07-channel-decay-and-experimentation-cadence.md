# Channel Decay and the Experimentation Cadence — the Founder Job is Not to Find a Magic Channel

## Motivation

The founder who finds a working primary channel in Q1 discovers a hard fact by Q4 of the following year: **the channel that produced a 22% reply rate on outbound in Q1 is producing a 9% reply rate on the same lists with the same cadence, and the CAC has drifted from $8K to $17K**. Nothing broke. The team did not stop trying. The AE is running the cadence with the same discipline. The list-building process is the same. The reply rate simply *decayed*.

Or: **the inbound pillar page that ranked top-3 on the target query and drove 400 sign-ups a month in year two is now ranked #8, drives 90 sign-ups a month, and is being overtaken by a competitor's more recent piece that shipped six weeks ago with a first-hand-data claim the original piece cannot match**. Nothing broke on the operating side. The piece is still well-written and technically correct. The channel simply *decayed*.

Or: **the LinkedIn Ads targeting that produced a $180 CPA in month 3 is producing a $520 CPA in month 12, because two better-funded competitors joined the same targeting set and the auction inflated**. The bidding is more efficient than it was; the audience is the same size; the creative is fresher. The channel-market fit decayed because the *market for the channel* changed.

None of these outcomes is a failure of execution. All of them are inevitable. Andrew Chen wrote the canonical description of the phenomenon in 2012 — the **"law of shitty click-throughs"**: **every marketing channel, over time, gets worse. The best time to run a channel was two years ago; the worst time is right before it saturates for everyone else. The founder's job is not to find a magic channel that lasts forever; the founder's job is to run a channel-experimentation cadence that produces the next working channel *before* the current one becomes unaffordable** ([Andrew Chen — "The Law of Shitty Clickthroughs"](https://andrewchen.com/the-law-of-shitty-clickthroughs/), 2012).

The failure mode this chapter exists to catch: **the founder finds one working channel in year one, treats it as the durable engine of the business, invests the entire go-to-market budget into scaling it through year two, watches the channel decay in year three, and has no second channel prepared to take over — because no channel-experimentation cadence was ever installed. The company enters year four running a decaying primary at deteriorating economics with no candidate replacement, and the fundraise conversation gets much harder.**

This chapter closes the module. It names why every channel decays (three mechanisms, each with its own diagnostic), develops the **channel-experimentation cadence** that keeps the primary + experimental portfolio in continuous rotation (the mechanism Chapter 1's doctrine implicitly assumes), and lays out the **cross-module operating rhythm** — how mod-108 interacts quarter-by-quarter with the mod-104 ICP audit, the mod-106 pricing pack change, the mod-107 motion diagnosis, and the mod-109 retention-loop instrumentation. The chapter's core operational output is a **quarterly channel review** the founder runs — the ritual that turns the doctrine into practice.

## Core concepts

### The three decay mechanisms

Every channel decays. The decay is not uniform across channels — each channel has a dominant decay mechanism, and understanding the mechanism is what tells the founder whether the fix is within-channel iteration, structural repositioning, or channel replacement.

**Mechanism 1 — auction inflation (paid, and paid-adjacent).** The dominant decay mechanism for paid acquisition. As more advertisers join a keyword set or targeting definition, the auction inflates, CPCs rise, and the CPA follows. The mechanism is almost purely structural — it has nothing to do with the founder's execution. Google Ads on "monorepo tooling" was cheap in 2018 and expensive in 2024 because eight competitors joined the auction in the interim. LinkedIn Ads on "VP Engineering at 500-employee SaaS" was cheap in 2019 and expensive in 2024 because every dev-tools company started buying that targeting. The founder-observable symptom: **CPC rises quarter-over-quarter on the same targeting** without any change in creative or targeting; CPA rises even when conversion rate holds; the platform's benchmark tools report the competitive-intensity metric climbing.

Auction-inflation decay is fastest and most predictable of the three mechanisms. It can be modelled forward — if the CPC is climbing at 8% per quarter and the payback envelope allows for a $40 CPC ceiling, the runway on the channel at that ceiling is calculable months in advance. Auction inflation is why paid at any given targeting is almost never a durable long-term primary — the channel eventually prices itself out.

**Mechanism 2 — audience saturation (all channels, most-visible for outbound and community).** As the founder runs a channel long enough, she saturates the reachable audience. The 300-account target list for outbound depletes as accounts either become customers, become explicit no-thanks, or reach cadence fatigue. The 15-person early-adopter cohort loses one member every few quarters to a job change or a product-fit shift, and the pool of new fit customers to invite has to be replenished from the paid customer base's growth. The Hacker News audience that clicked on the first three pillar pieces is now familiar with the founder's writing and clicks at half the rate on the fourth.

Audience saturation is slower than auction inflation but often deeper — it can be diagnostically invisible for a quarter or two while the reachable audience is still recovering (new companies join the ICP, new members join the community). The founder-observable symptom: **the reply rate on the same signal-selection criteria decays quarter-over-quarter**; the new-member acquisition rate for the community falls off; the article-to-signup conversion on the same-format pillar piece declines even as SEO position is unchanged.

Audience-saturation decay usually surfaces first on the top-of-funnel metric (fewer replies, fewer sign-ups) before it hits the closed-won metric. It is a slower crisis but a real one — the channel is producing fewer opportunities per unit of effort, and the CAC will climb whether or not the executed cadence is tighter than before.

**Mechanism 3 — market imitation and platform response (all channels, most-visible for inbound and community).** Competitors copy what works. When Loomly's stale-service-detection pillar page ranks top-3 in year 2, three competitors publish comparable pieces in year 3; the two better-funded ones out-invest Loomly on backlinks and internal-linking depth; Loomly's ranking drops to #6 not because the piece got worse but because the competitive set improved. Platforms respond to what works — Google's algorithm updates promote whatever competitor architecture aligns best with the newest ranking factor; the LLM answer engines cite whichever three sources the training crawl weighted highest, and that changes as the sources' domain authority shifts. The founder-observable symptom: **ranking position drifts on unchanged content**; a Hacker News post that would have hit the front page in 2022 stays in the New queue in 2024; a LinkedIn thought-leadership piece that would have gone viral 12 months ago now gets 300 impressions.

Market-imitation decay is the slowest and hardest to distinguish from execution failure — the founder can plausibly blame declining engagement on team turnover, on positioning drift, on any number of within-channel factors. The correct diagnostic is the *comparison to peer sources*: if the founder's inbound is declining but competitors' inbound is also declining, the decay is market-wide; if competitors' inbound is holding or growing, the decay is founder-specific and probably an execution problem.

**The compound decay problem.** In practice, channels usually decay via a combination of the three mechanisms simultaneously, and unpicking which mechanism dominates is what tells the founder what to do. **Auction-inflation decay** responds to bidding-strategy changes or new keyword sets (or to leaving the auction); **audience-saturation decay** responds to widening the list criteria or opening adjacent audiences; **market-imitation decay** responds to increasing the differentiation of the artifact (10× rewrite of a pillar page; new angle on the outbound cadence; new topical cluster) or to accepting that the channel is entering its declining phase and rotating a new one in.

### The half-life view — every channel has a productive window

Andrew Chen's canonical framing implies a **half-life view of channels**: every channel, viewed across a long-enough time horizon, produces most of its lifetime value in a **finite productive window**, then declines. The window is longer for some channels than others (inbound content on a well-maintained cluster can produce for 5-10 years; a specific paid targeting on a specific keyword may produce for 12-24 months; a viral referral loop may produce for 3-6 months before saturating). But every window ends.

Working half-life estimates (calibrated against the practitioner literature — working defaults, not laws; every ICP + product combination shifts them):

| Channel / sub-play | Typical productive window | Dominant decay mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Branded-defence paid** | Indefinite (as long as the brand has enough search volume to defend) | Market imitation (competitors bidding your brand — offset by continued defence) |
| **Google Ads on transactional-intent queries** | 12-24 months | Auction inflation |
| **LinkedIn Ads on targeted ICP** | 6-18 months | Auction inflation + audience saturation |
| **Meta Ads on look-alike audiences** | 3-12 months | Auction inflation + saturation |
| **Founder-led outbound to a specific segment** | 18-36 months | Audience saturation |
| **SDR-AE outbound (scale motion)** | 24-48 months | Audience saturation + cadence-response decay |
| **Inbound content on a well-maintained cluster** | 5-10 years (per cluster; individual pieces decay faster and need refresh) | Market imitation + platform response |
| **Individual inbound piece before refresh** | 12-36 months | Market imitation + platform response |
| **Community sub-motion 1 (early-adopter cohort)** | 12-24 months (cohort composition rotates; the sub-motion continues as the cohort turns over) | Audience saturation (cohort's founders move on) |
| **Community sub-motion 2 (public community-of-practice)** | 5+ years | Slowest to decay; requires ongoing founder / manager investment to sustain |
| **Partnerships / channel with a specific partner** | 12-36 months | Partner's own decay + relationship half-life |
| **Product Hunt / Show HN launch** | Single-event | Not a sustaining channel; a spike |
| **Conference / event participation (single event)** | 3-9 months of downstream pipeline | Not a sustaining channel; a spike |

The table has one uncomfortable implication: **most paid and most single-play channels have half-lives of 12-36 months.** A company banking on a single paid channel or a single outbound segment as the durable engine of the business will have to reinvent that channel every 1-3 years. Inbound content and public community-of-practice are the two outliers — they compound over 5-10 years but require the longest time-to-signal (Chapters 3 and 5) and the most sustained investment.

The right response is not to seek the un-decayable channel (there isn't one). The right response is to **install the channel-experimentation cadence that keeps the primary + experimental portfolio rotating** so that when the current primary reaches the shoulder of its productive window, an experimental has already matured into the next primary.

### The channel-experimentation cadence — the operational rhythm

Chapter 1 named the primary + experimental doctrine. This chapter names the **cadence** that keeps the doctrine in continuous operation. The cadence has three time horizons.

**Horizon 1 — weekly cadence review.** The founder (or the founder + first sales hire + first content hire) reviews the primary channel's Chapter 2 four-gate dashboard weekly during ramp, then bi-weekly / monthly once the primary is stable. The review answers three questions: **is the primary on-plan?** (four gates green), **is the experimental generating a testable signal?** (Gate 1 + Gate 4 readable at 6-8 weeks), **is any signal in Chapter 7's decay-mechanism table showing up in the primary?** (CPC drift, reply-rate decay, ranking drift). The weekly cadence catches execution issues; the higher-horizon cadences catch structural issues.

**Horizon 2 — quarterly channel review.** The founder runs a formal channel review once per quarter — a half-day working session with a written output. This is the load-bearing ritual of the doctrine. The quarterly review has five agenda items:

1. **Primary channel gate audit.** Full four-gate read on the primary channel with the last-90-day dataset. Are all four green? If any is amber or red, is the failure a fixable within-channel issue or a decay-mechanism signal?
2. **Experimental channel gate audit + graduation decision.** Same four-gate read on the experimental. Has the experimental accumulated enough signal to inform a graduation decision (become the next primary, remain experimental for another quarter, get demoted to "not now")?
3. **Decay-mechanism check on the primary.** Against the three mechanisms (auction inflation, audience saturation, market imitation), is any of them visibly firing on the primary channel? If yes, is the primary approaching the shoulder of its productive window? If yes, does the current experimental have a plausible path to become the next primary before the primary crosses into unaffordable territory?
4. **"Not now" list re-evaluation.** Every channel deferred to the "not now" list has a re-evaluation date. Which ones are due this quarter? Have their pre-conditions been met (mod-102 PMF signal, 100 paying customers, Series A close, ACV expansion into a new band)? Is any "not now" channel now ready to move into the experimental slot?
5. **Next-quarter portfolio decision.** Given the four inputs above, does the primary + experimental portfolio need to change for the next quarter? If yes, name the change explicitly, document the rationale, and update the operating dashboards. If no, name that explicitly too — a decision to hold the portfolio constant is a decision.

The quarterly review is written up as a 1-2 page memo — the format Chapter 1's Loomly example implies. Six memos across 18 months build a running history of the portfolio's evolution, which is what makes the next quarter's decisions defensible.

**Horizon 3 — annual channel-strategy re-derivation.** Once per year the founder (or founder + leadership team, at Series A+) re-runs the Chapter 1 primary-selection three-question test from scratch. The re-derivation checks whether the portfolio the last four quarterly reviews has evolved into is still the *correct* portfolio for the ICP + ACV + motion combination at its current shape. The re-derivation catches the slow drift that quarterly reviews miss — an ICP has drifted per mod-104 Chapter 7, an ACV band has moved per mod-106 Chapter 5, a motion has changed per mod-107 Chapter 6 — and each drift may invalidate a channel choice that was correct at the last derivation.

The annual re-derivation is what prevents the failure mode this chapter opens with: **running a decaying primary into year four because "we chose it in year one and we've always run it".** The re-derivation is not automatic; it has to be scheduled and adhered to.

### When to swap primary and experimental — the graduation rule

The most consequential decision in the quarterly review is the **graduation rule**: when to swap the primary and the experimental. The rule has three conditions; all three should be met before the swap.

**Condition 1 — the experimental has demonstrated four-gate parity or better against the primary.** Gate 1 (ICP-fit) is at the primary's level or higher; Gate 2 (unit economics) is inside the payback envelope and CAC : LTV ratio is comparable; Gate 3 (time-to-signal) has cleared the defensible-sample threshold; Gate 4 (attribution) is clean. The experimental is not a candidate primary if any of these is unproven.

**Condition 2 — the primary is showing decay signals that will make it under-perform the experimental within the next 2-4 quarters.** Auction inflation with a forward-projected CPA above the envelope; audience saturation with reply-rate decay quarter-over-quarter; market imitation with ranking drift on unchanged content. The decay is not necessarily happening *now*; the forward projection makes the swap the right decision *now* so that the transition happens before the primary crosses into unaffordable territory.

**Condition 3 — the operational transition is feasible in the next quarter.** Swapping primary and experimental has real operational cost — team focus shifts, tooling shifts, instrumentation shifts, hiring plans shift. If the team cannot absorb the transition in the next quarter (mid-hiring-cycle, mid-motion-pivot, mid-fundraise), the swap should be scheduled for the following quarter with the experimental continuing to grow into readiness.

When all three conditions are met, the swap is executed at the beginning of the next quarter with a formal announcement to the team and a written operating-plan update. The graduated channel becomes the primary; the former primary either continues in a reduced role (still producing revenue but at diminishing investment) or is demoted to "maintain" (kept alive, not scaled). A new experimental is selected against the Chapter 1 three-question test on the residual "not now" list.

When only one or two of the three conditions are met, the swap is *deferred to the next quarterly review*, and the operating plan is adjusted to move the incomplete condition into readiness (accelerate the experimental's remaining gate; instrument the primary's forward-decay signal; schedule the operational transition).

### The always-experimental slot — why it matters

The most common way the doctrine breaks in practice is not that the founder chooses the wrong experimental — it is that **the founder lets the experimental slot go empty for a quarter while the primary is doing well**. The reasoning is human and defensible: "the primary is working, the team is stretched, we don't need to be running experiments right now." Two quarters later, the primary starts decaying, and there is no experimental channel that has been running long enough to be the next primary.

The always-experimental slot is a **discipline**, not an optimisation. Even in quarters when the primary is doing well and the team is focused, the experimental slot has an owner, a budget, and a set of gate-checkpoint dates. The experimental may be running at deliberately-modest investment during easy quarters — but it is *running*, and its Chapter 2 gate reads are getting captured. The alternative is a bare experimental slot that leaves the founder with no candidate replacement when the primary begins to decay.

The experimental slot has one additional job the primary does not: **it is the founder's ongoing check on whether her theories about the channel-market fit are still calibrated**. A founder who ran only one primary for two years has strong evidence about that one channel and near-zero evidence about anything else — every strategic decision about the next channel is speculation. A founder who has run a rotating set of experimentals across those same two years has built up direct evidence about which non-primary channels do and do not work for this ICP + ACV + motion combination, and the next primary decision is grounded in that evidence.

### Cross-module operating rhythm — how mod-108 interacts with the rest of the track

The channel portfolio is not the only thing that evolves quarter-by-quarter. Six other modules produce updates that the channel review has to consume, and one module (mod-109) consumes the channel review's output. The cross-module rhythm:

- **mod-104 (ICP + Buyer Personas) — quarterly ICP audit output feeds the quarterly channel review's decay-mechanism check.** If mod-104's Chapter 7 diagnosis finds the ICP has drifted (new firmographic band, new champion persona, new geography), the current primary channel may need to change even if its four-gate reads are green — because the ICP the channel is reaching is no longer the ICP the pricing pack and motion serve. The mod-104 audit precedes the mod-108 quarterly review by 1-2 weeks so that the ICP drift is on the table.
- **mod-106 (Pricing and Packaging) — pack changes force channel-market-fit re-checks.** A pricing pack change (mod-106 Chapter 5) can invalidate a channel choice — a per-outcome pricing metric on a paid channel that assumed per-seat conversion characteristics stops working; a new enterprise tier opens a new ICP band that the current primary channel does not reach. The mod-108 quarterly review reads the mod-106 pack-change log; any pack change in the previous quarter triggers a channel-market-fit re-check in the current review.
- **mod-107 (Sales Motion Design) — motion changes force channel choice changes.** A motion pivot (mod-107 Chapter 6) changes the shape of leads the channel needs to produce. A pivot from SDR-AE to PLG changes the primary channel from outbound to inbound + community; a pivot from mid-market to enterprise changes the primary channel from targeted-outbound to partnerships + events. The mod-108 quarterly review is often *downstream* of a mod-107 motion diagnosis; if the motion is changing, the channel portfolio must be re-derived.
- **mod-105 (Founder-Led Sales) — the founder-led signal remains the strongest primary-channel candidate at any stage.** Even at Series A+ scale, when founder-led sales has transitioned to an SDR-AE motion, the founder should still be running some fraction of the ICP top-tier personally. That fraction is the ongoing source of first-hand pattern knowledge that the channel review consumes when re-evaluating primary and experimental choices.
- **mod-109 (Retention, Expansion, and Growth Loops) — the channel review's output feeds the retention-loop instrumentation.** Chapter 6 named the retention parity check (Gate 1C) as the diagnostic on whether a paid or scaled channel is producing customers that retain. mod-109 owns the retention-loop measurement; the channel review provides the cohort tags (channel-of-first-touch) that mod-109 uses to compute per-channel retention deltas. Without those cohort tags flowing quarter-over-quarter, the channel review cannot honestly complete Gate 1C.
- **mod-110 (GTM Metrics and First Hires) — first-hire decisions are downstream of channel-portfolio stability.** Hiring a full-time content lead, a first SDR, a first paid-marketing hire — each is a strategic bet on a channel being the primary for at least the ramp period (6-18 months) of the hire. The mod-108 quarterly review is the input to mod-110's hiring decisions; a channel that is showing decay signals is not a channel to hire against.

The rhythm compounds. A founder who runs quarterly channel reviews without the mod-104 ICP audit, the mod-106 pack-change log, and the mod-107 motion diagnosis is running the review on incomplete information. A founder who runs the mod-104 / mod-106 / mod-107 diagnostics without feeding them into the channel review is producing insights that never turn into portfolio changes. The rhythm is what makes the operating loop close.

### The failure mode of over-experimentation

A parallel failure to under-experimentation deserves naming: **the founder who runs the experimental slot but rotates the experimental every quarter, never letting any experimental reach the time-to-signal window that would produce a defensible answer**. This looks like discipline (experiments are running, quarterly reviews are happening, memos are being written) but functions like chaos (no experiment produces a signal; every quarter the portfolio is destabilised by a new "we're pivoting the experimental").

The correct discipline: **experiments run for at least their time-to-signal window (Chapter 2 Gate 3) before the results are read.** For paid, that is 12-24 weeks; for inbound, 6-12 months; for community, 12-24 months. An experimental that gets rotated at 6 weeks because "it's not producing yet" is a diagnostic-invisible experiment (the window did not open), and the founder learns nothing from it. The one-experimental-per-quarter reflex breaks the time-to-signal discipline; the correct default is **one experimental per productive window** — a paid experiment can rotate every 4-8 weeks within a single "paid experimentation" slot; a full inbound cluster experiment cannot rotate at that cadence and will occupy the experimental slot for 6-12 months.

The over-experimentation failure and the under-experimentation failure are the same failure viewed from opposite sides: **failing to hold the experimental slot at the correct cadence for the channel type being tested**. The remedy is the quarterly review's discipline of naming, for each experimental, its time-to-signal window and its next-checkpoint date — and only reading results (and only rotating the slot) against those checkpoints.

### The strategic patience the doctrine requires

The three-horizon cadence (weekly, quarterly, annual) requires a particular kind of strategic patience that founders often struggle to hold. The weekly cadence is easy — dashboards are visible and the operational levers are close at hand. The quarterly review is medium-hard — the founder has to *stop* for a half-day of retrospection and portfolio-decision, which the operational pressures of the week naturally resist. The annual re-derivation is the hardest — a full re-derivation feels like admitting the last year's decisions might have been wrong, which they usually partially were.

The patience is worth the cost because the alternative is a company that runs one channel until it dies and has no next channel prepared. Every quarterly review is cheap; every un-reviewed quarter is expensive; every year of un-audited decision is very expensive. The founder who installs the cadence in the first year builds an operating discipline that compounds through every subsequent year of the company's life; the founder who does not install it re-learns the doctrine painfully every 18-24 months when the current primary decays.

## Concrete example — Loomly across four quarterly reviews and one annual re-derivation

Loomly's portfolio evolves across 15 months of quarterly reviews. This is the compressed sequence.

**Q1 review (end of Q1).** Primary: founder-led outbound (Chapter 4). Experimental: inbound content + SEO (Chapter 3). Gate audit: outbound is amber-red on Gate 2 (CAC) contaminated by Gate 3 (small sample); inbound is red on Gate 3 (thin sample), green on Gates 1 and 4. Decay check: no visible decay signals on outbound (too early). Not-now list: paid re-evaluation postponed to Q2 for a bounded test; community deferred to 100 paying customers; partnerships deferred to Series A; events deferred to Series A. Portfolio decision for Q2: **hold the primary + experimental, extend the outbound cadence window without adding SDR headcount, extend the inbound cadence at Q1 investment level, add a paid learning-motion slot for branded-defence and a bounded LinkedIn test.**

**Q2 review (end of Q2).** Primary: founder-led outbound. Experimental: inbound content + SEO. Paid learning-motion: running (Chapter 6 example). Gate audit: outbound is now green on Gates 1, 3, and 4; Gate 2 CAC has improved to $18K (inside the healthy band) as the sample size grew to 15 closed deals; the CAC efficiency improvement came from moving Jane's Level-3 tier work to the AE and freeing Jane's hours for inbound and paid. Inbound is now amber on Gate 3 (approaching but not yet at the defensible-sample threshold), green on Gates 1, 2, and 4 for the sample that exists; two pillar-cluster pieces are ranking top-10 and beginning to compound. Paid is producing partial-signal, extended for another 6 weeks. Decay check: no visible decay on outbound; auction-inflation not visible on the branded-defence campaign. Portfolio decision for Q3: **hold the primary, promote inbound from experimental to co-primary starting Q3 (inbound has cleared enough gates and is showing compounding signals), demote paid to "learning-motion" slot (not experimental) with a rotating hypothesis, add community sub-motion 1 (early-adopter cohort) as the new experimental — the Q1 deferral to 100 paying customers is now met (~29 customers is close enough to seed the cohort).**

**Q3 review (end of Q3).** Primary A: founder-led outbound. Primary B: inbound content + SEO. Experimental: community (early-adopter cohort). Paid: branded-defence persistent, LinkedIn ICP-test succeeded partial-signal and graduated to a "maintain at $8K/month" investment level. Gate audit: both primaries green across all four gates on defensible samples. Cohort is producing early Signal (Chapter 5 metrics — active-member ratio 87%, reply-to-post 82%). Decay check: outbound reply rate on top-30 tier has dropped from 22% in Q1 to 18% in Q3 — audience-saturation decay beginning; mid-tier reply rate unchanged. Portfolio decision for Q4: **hold the primary A + primary B combination; keep community in the experimental slot (still inside its 6-24 month window); refresh the outbound target-account list criteria to bring in adjacent-signal accounts (Category B expansion — GitLab-based orgs, not just GitHub-based) to counter the audience-saturation decay.**

**Q4 review (end of Q4).** Primary A: founder-led outbound (with expanded list criteria). Primary B: inbound. Experimental: community cohort (continuing). Gate audit: outbound is holding at 18% reply rate on the expanded list; inbound is now driving 40% of new-logo pipeline through Chapter 3's compounding; community cohort is producing referrals at rate that will feed Q1 next-year revenue. Paid maintenance stable. Decay check: LinkedIn Ads CPC has drifted from $1.78 in Q2 to $2.90 in Q4 — auction inflation firing; still inside the payback envelope but on-track to cross the ceiling in 2-3 quarters if the drift continues. Portfolio decision for Q1-next-year: **maintain the same portfolio one more quarter; prepare a Q2-next-year rotation where LinkedIn Ads is demoted from maintenance to "hypothesis retirement" and community cohort's public-community-of-practice extension (Chapter 5 sub-motion 2) becomes the new experimental.**

**Annual re-derivation (end of Q4).** Jane runs the full Chapter 1 three-question test with the updated ICP (mod-104 audit finds no drift), pricing pack (mod-106 audit finds a new enterprise tier added, opening a new $50-100K ACV band on the Fortune-500-adjacent list), motion (mod-107 audit finds partial Mismatch 2 on enterprise-layer top-of-funnel from Loomly's mod-107 Chapter 6 example, which is now being addressed with a new enterprise SDR hire). Re-derivation output: **the primary A + primary B portfolio remains correct for the mid-market motion; a new experimental for the enterprise layer — partnerships / channel through systems-integrator relationships — is added for the following year; the "not now" list is re-dated with new re-evaluation triggers.**

The five reviews took, in total, roughly 6 hours of Jane's time across the year plus a half-day annual re-derivation — 10 hours of founder time to keep the channel portfolio in continuous rotation. The alternative — running Q1's outbound + inbound portfolio unchanged through the year and re-evaluating only when the outbound audience-saturation decay became a crisis — would have surrendered 6-12 months of preparation for the community and partnerships channels that turned out to be the next primaries.

## Common failure patterns

- **One-channel-forever trap.** Founder finds a working primary in year one, invests entirely into it through year two, watches it decay in year three, has no candidate replacement. Fix: install the primary + experimental doctrine from Chapter 1 in year one; hold the experimental slot even during easy quarters.
- **Skip-the-quarterly-review trap.** Founder runs weekly cadence reviews but never schedules the quarterly. Decay signals appear on quarterly-cadence dashboards that weekly dashboards do not resolve. Fix: quarterly review as a scheduled calendar ritual, half-day, half-day-only, with a written memo output.
- **Skip-the-annual-re-derivation trap.** Founder runs quarterly reviews but never runs the annual re-derivation. Slow drift accumulates: the ICP has moved, the pricing has moved, the motion has moved, but the channel portfolio was chosen against the year-old inputs. Fix: annual re-derivation is scheduled and adhered to; it is the ritual that catches slow drift the quarterly cadence misses.
- **Empty-experimental-slot trap.** Founder lets the experimental slot go empty during quarters when the primary is doing well. Two quarters later the primary begins decaying and there is no candidate replacement. Fix: experimental slot is always filled — even at modest investment — with an owner, a budget, and a set of gate-checkpoint dates.
- **Rotate-experimental-every-quarter trap.** Founder rotates the experimental every quarter without letting any experimental reach its time-to-signal window. Discipline appearance, chaos reality. Fix: experimental runs for its time-to-signal window (Chapter 2 Gate 3); rotation is scheduled against the window, not the calendar.
- **Confuse-execution-failure-with-channel-decay trap.** Reply rate on outbound drops from 22% to 12%; founder concludes "outbound is decaying" and pivots to a new experimental. Actual answer: the AE left three months ago and the replacement is running the cadence at 60% quality. Fix: the decay-mechanism check compares to peer channels and to the specific decay signals (CPC drift, saturation-on-unchanged-list); execution-failure signals look different (specific stage-of-the-funnel deterioration, specific team-change coincidence).
- **Confuse-channel-decay-with-execution-failure trap.** The mirror of the above: reply rate on outbound drops from 22% to 12%; founder concludes "we need to hire another SDR" and doubles down on execution. Actual answer: audience-saturation decay on the list; more SDRs against the same list will not produce more replies. Fix: read the specific decay signals; if audience-saturation is firing, widen the list criteria before adding capacity.
- **Modelled-decay-ignored trap.** LinkedIn CPC is climbing at 15% per quarter; founder holds the LinkedIn campaign because it is currently inside the payback envelope; two quarters later CPC has crossed the envelope and the founder is scrambling to demobilise. Fix: forward-project the decay trajectory; make the exit or transition decision *before* the channel crosses into unaffordable territory.
- **Refresh-cluster-too-late trap.** Inbound pillar page ranks #3 in year 2; competitor's newer piece ranks #5 in year 2 and climbs to #2 in year 3 as the original piece ages. Founder never scheduled a refresh cycle; the piece stays as-written until it drops to #8 and loses most of its traffic. Fix: quarterly refresh audit on top-20 inbound pieces; refresh-or-deprecate decision each quarter (Chapter 3 refresh discipline).
- **Cross-module-signal-ignored trap.** mod-104 audit finds the ICP has drifted; mod-108 review does not receive the audit output; channel portfolio continues targeting the old ICP for another 6 months; both audits produce insights that never turn into portfolio changes. Fix: cross-module rhythm — mod-104 audit precedes mod-108 review; mod-106 and mod-107 diagnoses feed mod-108's inputs; mod-108's channel-cohort tags feed mod-109's retention analysis.
- **Community-experiment-cancelled-during-crisis trap.** Runway shortens, founder cancels the "slow-to-signal" community experimental to focus on the primary; 18 months of community investment lost; when the primary decays 2 quarters later, community is no longer 6 months from readiness — it is at zero. Fix: community's slow-to-signal window makes it especially important not to demote during short-term pressure; if runway pressures force demotions, demote paid or single-quarter experiments first, preserve long-window investments.
- **Never-hire-against-a-decaying-channel trap-violation.** Founder hires a full-time content lead as the outbound-experimental transitions to primary; content is decaying at 15% year-over-year on the current cluster; hire ramps into a channel that will be a diminishing return in year 2. Fix: mod-108 quarterly review feeds mod-110 hiring decisions; do not hire against a channel that is showing decay signals unless the hire is explicitly for the refresh / renewal work.
- **Fundraise-narrative-freezes-the-portfolio trap.** Founder builds a Series A narrative around "our winning outbound motion"; portfolio cannot be updated during the fundraise because the deck is out; three quarters pass with the portfolio frozen while the outbound decays underneath the narrative. Fix: fundraise narratives describe the operating discipline (the primary + experimental doctrine, the quarterly review, the running experimental) rather than a specific static portfolio; the discipline is durable, individual channel choices are not.

## Summary

- **Every channel decays.** Andrew Chen's "law of shitty click-throughs" is the canonical framing. The founder's job is not to find a magic channel that lasts forever — it is to install the **channel-experimentation cadence** that produces the next working channel before the current one becomes unaffordable.
- **Three decay mechanisms**: **auction inflation** (paid; the dominant mechanism; forward-modellable), **audience saturation** (all channels; slower; hits top-of-funnel first), **market imitation and platform response** (all channels; hardest to distinguish from execution failure; diagnosed by comparison to peer channels).
- **Every channel has a productive window** (a half-life). Working defaults: paid targetings 12-24 months, outbound segments 18-36 months, individual inbound pieces 12-36 months (clusters 5-10 years with refresh), public community-of-practice 5+ years. Most sustaining channels have half-lives of 12-36 months.
- **The three-horizon cadence**: **weekly** four-gate dashboard review; **quarterly** formal channel review (five agenda items: primary audit, experimental audit + graduation decision, decay check, not-now re-evaluation, next-quarter decision); **annual** three-question re-derivation from scratch.
- **The graduation rule**: swap primary and experimental when **(1) the experimental has demonstrated four-gate parity or better**, **(2) the primary is showing decay signals that will out-pace the experimental within 2-4 quarters**, **(3) the operational transition is feasible next quarter**. All three or defer.
- **The always-experimental slot** is a discipline, not an optimisation. Even during easy quarters the experimental has an owner, a budget, and a checkpoint calendar. The alternative is a bare slot when the primary starts to decay.
- **Over-experimentation is the mirror failure of under-experimentation**: rotating the experimental every quarter breaks the time-to-signal discipline; each channel must run for its window before the read. **One experimental per productive window**, not one experimental per quarter.
- **Cross-module rhythm**: mod-104 ICP audit precedes mod-108 quarterly review; mod-106 pack changes trigger channel-market-fit re-checks; mod-107 motion pivots force channel-portfolio re-derivation; mod-108 cohort tags feed mod-109 retention analysis; mod-108 quarterly review is the input to mod-110 hiring decisions.
- **Strategic patience is the cost.** Weekly is easy; quarterly is medium-hard (requires stopping); annual is hardest (requires admitting last year's decisions may have partially wrong). All three horizons are worth the cost — every un-reviewed quarter is expensive; every un-audited year is very expensive.
- The module closes with the **operating loop**: Chapter 1 sets the doctrine (primary + experimental); Chapter 2 diagnoses each channel (four gates); Chapters 3-6 develop the archetypes (inbound / outbound / community / paid); Chapter 7 installs the cadence that keeps the doctrine running quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year. The exercises drill each piece; the module lab assembles them into a working channel portfolio + quarterly-review artifact for one startup.
